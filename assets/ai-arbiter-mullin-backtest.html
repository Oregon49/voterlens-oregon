Backtest — The Border Wall Stay

Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church, No. 26A560, 610 U.S. ___ (2026)
Per curiam, November 9, 2026. Application for stay filed October 21, 2026; response due October 29; reply October 30 — fully briefed. Referred to the Court and granted November 9.
Below: preliminary injunction, W.D. Tex. No. 3:26-cv-01099 (Judge Kathleen Cardone), October 2, 2026.

AI RULING: AGREE with the Court. GRANT the stay. The district court lacked jurisdiction over the challengers' statutory claim, so the injunction cannot stand on that ground. Predicted vote: 8–1 (Jackson would deny) — the same as the actual order. Comparison: AGREE.

The case, in one paragraph

The Secretary of Homeland Security issued three waivers (February–July 2026) under the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act (IIRIRA) §102, waiving legal requirements to build border barriers and roads in the Big Bend sector of Texas. Construction began near Santa Elena Canyon on August 6 and paused August 17. Challengers sued; on October 2, Judge Cardone entered a preliminary injunction blocking the waivers and halting all construction. The Solicitor General asked the Supreme Court for either summary reversal or a stay. The Court granted the stay in a five-page per curiam and declined summary reversal. The stay lasts while the government seeks certiorari; it ends automatically if cert is denied. Justice Jackson would have denied the stay; no other justice noted disagreement, and no dissent was written.

Reasoning

  1. Jurisdiction first. IIRIRA §102 grants the Secretary broad authority to construct border barriers and roads "to deter illegal crossings in areas of high illegal entry." §102(c)(2)(C) restricts judicial review of the Secretary's waiver decisions to constitutional claims. The challengers' lead claim was a major-questions-doctrine claim — a statutory claim, not a constitutional one. The district court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate it. The text controls: where Congress has withdrawn jurisdiction over a class of claims, a court may not hear them.
  2. The stay factors (Nken v. Holder). (a) Likelihood of success: the government is exceedingly likely to prevail on the jurisdictional ground above. (b) Irreparable harm: the injunction bars the Secretary from exercising statutory waiver authority and halts construction Congress authorized. (c) Balance of equities and public interest: the government has a compelling interest in border security, and the challengers lose nothing they cannot still pursue — any genuine constitutional claim remains for a court with jurisdiction.
  3. What this ruling does NOT do. It is not a ruling on the merits of the waivers. It does not hold the waivers lawful. And it declines summary reversal, as the Court did — the jurisdictional holding disposes of the injunction without reaching the merits. The legality of the waivers remains undecided, by both the Court and this ruling.
  4. The lower court's order, scored. Judge Cardone's October 2 injunction was wrong on jurisdiction as to the statutory claim — the court lacked jurisdiction over the major-questions claim it rested on. Flag: the full injunction order was not independently read; this score rests on the per curiam's description of the claims. If constitutional claims were also pleaded, they would need a court with jurisdiction to hear them — that question is not decided here.

What the backtest shows

The emergency lane works on a short record with no oral argument: application, response, reply, lower-court order — and the framework produced the Court's answer (grant the stay, 8–1, on jurisdiction, summary reversal declined). Note what the Court's order did not contain: it was a five-page per curiam, and only Justice Jackson's position was noted. The AI ruling supplies the reasoning the order sketches — and says plainly where the order stops: the merits are undecided. That is the emergency lane's honest limit: the comparison is usually on the result and the vote, because the Court often gives little or no reasoning.
