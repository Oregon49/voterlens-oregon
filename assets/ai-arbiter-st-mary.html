AI Ruling — Test Case 1

St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, No. 25-581
Argued November 3, 2026 (2 hours). Undecided. Decision below: 154 F.4th 752 (10th Cir.). Certiorari granted April 20, 2026, limited to Questions 1 and 2.

HOLDING: REVERSED. Colorado's Universal Preschool Program may not exclude Catholic preschools from a generally available public benefit because their admissions practice is an exercise of religion. The exclusion violates the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The Tenth Circuit's judgment is reversed and the case is remanded for entry of an injunction. Predicted vote: 6–3 to reverse.

The case, in one paragraph

Colorado voters created a Universal Preschool Program (UPK) that pays for families to send their children to the preschool of their choice — public or private — up to 15 hours a week. To participate, a preschool must sign a nondiscrimination agreement: equal opportunity to enroll regardless of race, ethnicity, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity, lack of housing, income level, or disability. Colorado permits secular preferences — income, disability, IEP, Head Start, "children of color," "gender-nonconforming children," "the LGBTQ community" — and runs a matching algorithm with a discretionary "catchall" for individualized adjustments. St. Mary Catholic Parish (Littleton), St. Bernadette Catholic Parish (Lakewood), and the Archdiocese of Denver admit only families who support Catholic beliefs, including on sex and gender; in 2023 the Archdiocese directed its schools not to enroll rather than sign. They sued, saying the condition forces a choice between a public benefit and their faith. The district court denied an injunction and the Tenth Circuit affirmed, holding the requirement neutral and generally applicable under Employment Division v. Smith and subject only to rational-basis review. The Court took two questions — not the third (whether Smith should be overruled).

The questions the Court took

  1. Whether proving a lack of general applicability under Smith requires showing unfettered discretion or categorical exemptions for identical secular conduct.
  2. Whether Carson v. Makin displaces Smith only when the government explicitly excludes religious people and institutions.

Question 3 — whether Smith should be overruled — was NOT granted. This ruling does not overrule Smith.

Standing — satisfied, not the battleground

The parishes are excluded from UPK funding and the Sheley family cannot use the benefit at the school their children attend. The injury is concrete and particularized, traceable to Colorado's enforcement of the agreement, and redressable by an injunction. No justiciability bar applies. The fight is on the merits.

Reasoning

The framework this ruling was told to apply

Constitutional text and its original public meaning. Precedent is context, not the standard: it shows how the Court has read the text, but a precedent that departs from the text does not bind this ruling. The Federalist Papers, Madison's Memorial and Remonstrance (1785), Jefferson's Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (1786), and the First Congress debates are the founders' evidence of what "free exercise" meant. Citations must be checkable.

  1. The text. "Congress shall make no law… prohibiting the free exercise [of religion]." Exercise is the practice of religion, not belief alone. Madison's Memorial and Remonstrance (1785): religion is "wholly exempt from [civil society's] cognizance," and "the duty which we owe to our Creator" is "precedent, both in order of time and in degree of obligation, to the claims of Civil Society." Jefferson's Statute (1786): "no man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship… nor shall otherwise suffer on account of his religious opinions or belief." A law that makes a public benefit available only on condition of abandoning a religious practice makes the believer "suffer on account of" religion. The admissions practice here is the exercise: as counsel argued, the schools exist to pass the faith to the next generation, and they ask enrolling families to be open to and supportive of the Church's teachings. Conditioning UPK money on dropping that practice prohibits its free exercise.
  2. The benefits line. The Court's own public-benefits cases point the same way, and they are consistent with the text: Trinity Lutheran v. Comer (2017), Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue (2020), Carson v. Makin (2022). Carson held Maine could not exclude schools from tuition assistance "on the basis of their religious exercise." Colorado's program is the same shape — a generally available benefit, conditioned on surrendering a religious practice. The Tenth Circuit cabined Carson to exclusions "on the explicit basis" of religion. Carson's own reasoning looks to "operation and effect," not labels. The exclusion here operates on religious exercise, whatever Colorado calls it.
  3. General applicability — Question 1. Smith (1990) excuses only laws that are neutral and generally applicable. Fulton v. City of Philadelphia (2021) holds a law is not generally applicable when the government retains discretion to grant exemptions — the mere existence of a mechanism for individualized exemptions triggers strict scrutiny. Colorado's UPK has exactly that: a discretionary "catchall" and a matching algorithm that approves individualized preferences (income, disability, race, gender-nonconforming, LGBTQ community). The Tenth Circuit demanded proof of "unfettered discretion or categorical exemptions for identical secular conduct." That bar is higher than Fulton allows. A system that lets the state prefer secular groups while refusing any religious accommodation is not generally applicable — it is a system of individualized exemptions, and under Fulton strict scrutiny follows.
  4. Strict scrutiny — Colorado fails it. Equal access is a compelling interest in the abstract. But the law is not narrowly tailored: it permits secular preferences that undermine the very equal-access goal it claims (a school may prefer children of color or LGBTQ children, but not Catholic families), and it pursues that goal by excluding the families a "universal" program claims to serve. Under Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah (1993), a law that pursues its interest only against religious conduct is not neutral. Colorado has less restrictive ways to serve equal access — including funding families directly, as UPK already does, without dictating the admissions practices of religious schools.
  5. Carson's scope — Question 2. Carson displaces Smith whenever the government conditions a public benefit on surrendering religious exercise, not only when it says so explicitly. An explicit-exclusion rule would let any state evade Carson by writing the condition in neutral words while enforcing it only against religious conduct — which is what happened here. Justice Thomas noted at argument that Espinoza and Carson did not use or even cite Smith, suggesting Smith is out of play in the benefits context. This ruling agrees: in the public-benefits context, Carson governs.
  6. What this ruling does NOT do. It does not overrule Smith — Question 3 was not granted, and this ruling does not reach it. It does not hold that a state must fund religious discrimination in its own schools: the parishes remain free to discriminate on their own dime, and Colorado remains free to run its own preschools by its own rules. It does not decide the Free Speech or Establishment Clause claims raised below. And it does not rest on the Federalist Papers — they say little directly about free exercise (No. 10 on factions, No. 51 on the multiplicity of sects as a check). The founders' evidence on religious liberty lives in Madison's Memorial, Jefferson's Statute, and the First Congress debates, and that is what this ruling cites.

Predicted vote: 6–3 to reverse

The six-justice majority — Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett — has consistently applied the Carson / Trinity Lutheran line in the benefits cases, and the argument signaled the same direction: a majority of the Justices seemed inclined to side with the schools. The three dissenters — Sotomayor, Kagan, Jackson — would likely defend Smith and the state's equal-access interest. Justice Jackson's concern at argument — that the schools want to do what the state has determined is discrimination — previews the dissent's core. This vote is a prediction, not a ruling. The ruling is the holding above; the vote is a forecast of how the nine will line up under the same framework.
