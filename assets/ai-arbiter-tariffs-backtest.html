Backtest — The Tariff Case

Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump (consolidated with Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.), Nos. 24-1287 and 25-250, 607 U.S. 229 (2026)
Argued November 5, 2025 · Decided February 20, 2026 · Vote: 6–3 · Author: Roberts
Majority: Roberts, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, Jackson. Dissent: Kavanaugh (joined by Thomas and Alito); Thomas also dissented separately.

Holding: The International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. Statutory holding with constitutional avoidance — the Court did not strike IEEPA down.

Decisions below: Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, 784 F. Supp. 3d 209 (D.D.C. 2025) (preliminary injunction for the importers — IEEPA does not authorize tariffs); V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. United States, 772 F. Supp. 3d 1350 (Ct. Int'l Trade 2025) (summary judgment for five small businesses and 12 States), affirmed in relevant part, 149 F.4th 1312 (Fed. Cir. 2025) (en banc).

Disposition — read it carefully: the Court decided the merits through the companion case. The judgment in No. 25-250 (V.O.S. Selections) was affirmed; the judgment in No. 24-1287 (Learning Resources) was vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction — tariff cases belong exclusively in the Court of International Trade, 28 U.S.C. § 1581(i). The merits holding is unqualified; the named case was dismissed on jurisdiction, not on the merits.

Standing and justiciability

Standing is satisfied and was not the battleground. The plaintiffs are importers who paid the tariffs: injury (money paid), traceability (the tariffs were imposed under the challenged orders), redressability (a ruling for them means refunds). The justiciability point that mattered was jurisdiction, not standing — see the disposition above.

AI RULING: AGREE with the Court. The International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. Vote match: 6–3 — the same lineup as the actual decision. Comparison: AGREE.

Reasoning

  1. The text. "The Congress shall have Power To lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises…" (Article I, §8, cl. 1). A tariff is a duty — a tax on imported goods. The taxing power is vested in Congress alone. The President has no taxing power to exercise.
  2. "Regulate" is not "tax." IEEPA lets the President "regulate" importation in an emergency — 50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1)(B). The statute's own list of powers is nine verbs: "investigate, block during the pendency of an investigation, regulate, direct and compel, nullify, void, prevent or prohibit" — and eleven nouns describing the transactions. The words "tariff" and "duty" appear nowhere in it. In the Constitution's own vocabulary, regulating and taxing are distinct powers — Article I, §8 lists them in separate clauses (cl. 1 taxes, cl. 3 commerce). When Congress grants tariff authority, it says so expressly — using the words "duty" or "surcharge" — and sets procedural limits. IEEPA has neither word and neither limit. The Court's own line: "Based on two words separated by 16 others in Section 1702(a)(1)(B) of IEEPA — 'regulate' and 'importation' — the President asserts the independent power to impose tariffs on imports from any country, of any product, at any rate, for any amount of time. Those words cannot bear such weight." (slip op. at 5.)
  3. The Export Clause. "No Tax or Duty shall be laid on Articles exported from any State." (Article I, §9, cl. 5). IEEPA covers exports as well as imports — the same grant reaches "importation or exportation." Reading it to authorize tariffs would let the President lay duties on exports — the one thing the Constitution forbids outright. The government's reading would render IEEPA partially unconstitutional. (slip op. at 15.)
  4. The founders' evidence. Federalist No. 30 (Hamilton): the power of taxation belongs to the legislature, and it is the power that funds the government — it is not an executive power. And the founding generation's own practice settles it: the First Congress itself passed the Tariff Act of 1789, imposing duties by statute. Congress laid the tariffs; the President did not.
  5. The major-questions check — and its limit. A claim of power over the entire tariff system — hundreds of billions of dollars, every imported good — needs clear authorization. IEEPA has none. But say plainly how far this ground goes: Parts II-A-2 and III of the opinion — the major-questions reasoning — were a plurality, joined only by Gorsuch and Barrett. The opinion of the Court (Parts I, II-A-1, II-B) — the textual reasoning — commanded all six. The AI's core reasoning matches the part all six joined; the major-questions check is the narrower, 3-justice ground. (It is a check here, not the standard — the text decides it.)
  6. Precedent as context. The majority opinion decides this case on the statute's text; it does not rest on Youngstown. The framework notes Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952) (Jackson, J., concurring), as context: a President acting where Congress occupies the field is at the lowest ebb of his power — and Congress occupies the taxing field. Context, not the standard — the text decides it.
  7. What the dissent gets right, and where it fails. Justice Kavanaugh is right that "regulate" ordinarily has a broad meaning. But breadth is not the question. The question is whether Congress delegated the taxing power, and the Constitution says that power belongs to Congress alone — it cannot be handed over by a word as general as "regulate." Justice Thomas is right that the nondelegation doctrine may not apply to foreign commerce. But that answers whether Congress may delegate tariff power; the question here is whether it did. It did not.
  8. Scope. This is a statutory holding. IEEPA as written does not authorize tariffs; IEEPA itself is not struck down. Other tariff statutes — Section 232, Section 301 — are separate laws with their own text, and are not decided here. The Court did not rule on the replacement authorities either (see What happened next).

What happened next

Congressional Budget Office, Monthly Budget Review for July 2026 (August 10, 2026): about $100 billion in refunds had been issued for tariffs collected under the IEEPA authority. The administration ended the IEEPA tariffs, temporarily imposed new duties under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — which expired July 24, 2026 — and then imposed a new set under Section 301. CBO expects 2026 tariff collections about $250 billion below earlier projections. The Court ruled on none of those replacement authorities.

What the backtest shows
