Test Case 2 — Case File

Viramontes v. Cook County, No. 25-238, consolidated with Grant v. Higgins, No. 25-566
Certiorari granted June 30, 2026. Oral argument: Wednesday, December 2, 2026. Decision expected summer 2027.

The question

"Whether the Second and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee the right to possess AR-15 platform and similar semiautomatic rifles."

This is the first time since Heller (2008) that the Court will squarely address whether commonly owned semiautomatic rifles are protected "arms." It is the most significant Second Amendment case since Bruen (2022).

The case, in one paragraph

Cook County, Illinois has banned the possession, acquisition, and transfer of so-called assault weapons — including AR-15-style semiautomatic rifles — since 2013. Cutberto Viramontes, a Cook County resident, wanted to own a Smith & Wesson Military & Police AR-15-style rifle; Christopher Khaya wanted an IMI Galil. They sued with the Second Amendment Foundation and the Firearms Policy Coalition. The district court granted summary judgment for the county, holding under Bruen's first prong that assault weapons are not arms the Second Amendment protects. The Seventh Circuit affirmed in a nonprecedential decision (June 2, 2025), holding the plaintiffs had not shown that the Second Amendment, viewed through historical tradition, protected possession of assault weapons, and reiterating that semiautomatic rifles are not materially different from machineguns and military-grade weaponry. The consolidated case, Grant v. Higgins, challenges Connecticut's post-Sandy Hook ban on modern semiautomatic firearms, including the AR-15; the Second Circuit affirmed the denial of a preliminary injunction.

The lower courts' reasoning — and what the AI will test it against

  • Seventh Circuit (Viramontes): AR-15s are not "Arms" under the Second Amendment, following Bevis v. City of Naperville (7th Cir. 2023); semiautomatic rifles are not materially different from machineguns and military-grade weaponry, which Heller said can be banned.
  • The petitioners' argument: AR-15-style rifles are "arms" under the plain text, are in common use by millions of law-abiding Americans for self-defense, hunting, and target shooting, and cannot be categorically banned under Heller and Bruen.
  • The United States (Solicitor General, Sept 4, 2026): "the AR-15 rifle is unquestionably in common use among law-abiding citizens for lawful purposes," and "a ban on possessing such rifles thus violates the Second Amendment." The SG was granted divided argument time on Nov 9, 2026.
  • The counties' argument: these are military-style weapons outside the Second Amendment's protection, and the bans are consistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation — citing prohibitions on obscure weapons like launcegays and slungshots.

The mootness wrinkle — standing first

Live as of Nov 11, 2026, and the arbiter's first job on this case is standing, before the merits.

  • Oct 29, 2026: Viramontes moved to dismiss himself from the case, without a stated reason.
  • Nov 5, 2026: Cook County filed a Suggestion of Mootness — if Viramontes is dismissed, the remaining individual petitioner Khaya wants a Galil, not an AR-15-platform rifle, so the Cook County portion is moot or improvidently granted.
  • The response: the Second Amendment Foundation says the case should continue for Khaya and SAF's members; the county's own filing concedes in a footnote that the jurisdictional issue "would not prevent the Court from deciding the question presented" because Grant v. Higgins is consolidated. One analysis notes the county's own brief defines the ordinance's banned weapons as "AR-15 platform weapons" including AK-47 rifles — so Khaya's Galil is inside the question as the county itself framed it.
  • Status: as of Nov 9–10, the Court had not acted on either filing. Argument remains on the calendar for Dec 2. The question survives regardless through Grant.

Key precedent the ruling will work with

CaseWhat it says
District of Columbia v. Heller (2008)Second Amendment protects an individual right; limited to weapons "in common use at the time"; machineguns and military-grade weaponry can be banned.
McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010)The right applies against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.
Caetano v. Massachusetts (2016)Stun guns are arms; "common use" is not frozen at 1791.
New York State Rifle & Pistol Assn. v. Bruen (2022)Text, history, and tradition test — no interest-balancing. Step one: does the plain text cover the conduct? Step two: is the regulation consistent with the nation's historical tradition?
Bevis v. City of Naperville (7th Cir. 2023)The decision the Seventh Circuit relied on below: AR-15s are not "Arms."
Barnett v. Raoul (7th Cir. July 9, 2026)Seventh Circuit upheld Illinois's statewide ban (Protect Illinois Communities Act). The Viramontes ruling could change that outcome.

Founders' evidence the ruling will draw on

  • Text: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed." Fourteenth Amendment: no state shall deprive any person of liberty without due process of law.
  • Federalist No. 46 (Madison): the advantage of Americans over other countries is that they are armed — "the existence of subordinate governments, to which the people are attached, and by which the militia officers are appointed," plus "the advantage of being armed, which the Americans possess over the people of almost every other nation."
  • Federalist No. 29 (Hamilton): on the militia and "a well-regulated militia."
  • St. George Tucker, Blackstone's Commentaries (1803): the right to keep and bear arms as a check on standing armies and government overreach.
  • The historical-tradition question: what regulations existed at the founding and in the 19th century — the Bruen step-two inquiry. The counties cite obscure-weapon bans; the petitioners will cite the absence of any categorical ban on commonly owned rifles.
