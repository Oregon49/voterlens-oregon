Journalist Scoreboard

Liar, liar, pants on fire?

Sample scoreboard — Monday, November 9, 2026. A one-day sample, not a live-updating board. Nothing on this page is a verdict on any outlet, writer, or article.

Want to check a single journalist? Score a journalist — paste an article link or type a name.

What this board is testing

“The news source likes to word things so it’s hard to figure out the truth — whether it’s opinion or fact or what they really said or whatever. And if you’re trying to fool your audience then you’re a liar liar.” — Allan, November 9, 2026

That is the test this board runs. Not “did they get the facts right” — these checks can’t see that. The test is whether the outlet’s wording makes the truth hard to find: opinion dressed as fact, paraphrase standing in for what was actually said, a headline selling more than the story holds, claims resting on nobody you can check. Wording that shows you the truth scores high. Wording that hides it scores low.

What it does: for each outlet, on each of five checks, it shows whether the wording showed you the source material or handed you a conclusion — the quote next to the spin, the headline next to the story — so you can judge for yourself.

What it does not do: tell you what to think. Deliver a truth verdict on any story. Say whether the underlying facts are false. Score opinion pieces as if they were news — opinion is labeled as opinion, never scored as a fact claim.

Sample: 20 articles, Monday Nov 9, 2026, 16,791 words, all full text. Read via syndication partners — apnews.com blocks direct fetching, and MSN copies are JS shells with no extractable body. 10 of the 20 came via ABC’s wireStory feed (verbatim AP wire, moved out of ABC’s count).

Labeling (reference only — no longer part of Check 1): Audit 100. Publishes no opinion at all as a stated policy (apnews.com/purpose: “our mission is to produce factual journalism, not offer opinion”). No Opinion section exists to confuse with news; the distinction is structural, not per-article labeling. This audit now feeds the future Integrity score, not Check 1.

Corrections: No standalone public corrections page found. Corrections are appended to individual articles; errors can be reported via apnews.com/contact-us.

Notable: Two zero-quote briefs cap Check 2 at 25 (south-africa-war-recruits, migrant-boat-greece). Two Check 3 scores of 50 (france-iran-fridge-hack, germany-female-president).

How each check is scored

Same rule for every outlet. Countable things only. Every formula below is applied by the same automated scorer to the article text as fetched.

  • Check 1 — Loaded language (news copy only): 100 − 22 × loaded terms per 100 words, from a fixed 45-term watch list (slammed, blasted, regime, scheme, radical, shameless, chaos, cronies, witch hunt, and the rest). Changed in v3: Check 1 is now purely this loaded-language score. In v2 it was 70% loaded-language mean + 30% labeling audit; the labeling audit has moved out entirely (Allan, Nov 10, 2026) to the standalone Integrity score below.
  • Integrity score — standalone, rubric to design, NOT scored in this run. Allan’s decision, November 10, 2026: labeling comes out of Check 1 and becomes its own score. What it will measure: (a) how cleanly an outlet keeps opinion out of news and labels it when it is opinion — opinion labeled as opinion, kept in its own lane, earns integrity; a news or business piece that throws opinions in instead of facts loses it; (b) the speed incentive — rushing to be first, then letting the story evolve over hours as the facts come in. This run proves (b) is measurable: CNN headlines were rewritten the same day, and a Fox Business URL from the morning feed now serves a different Nov 9 story than the one it pointed to in the morning. The 48-hour re-fetch is the mechanism; its relationship to Check 4 (corrections watch) is still open.
  • Check 2 — Quote vs. paraphrase: starts at 50; +5 per direct quote (max +35); +5 per named “said [name/title]” attribution (max +15); capped at 25 if the article has no direct quotes at all.
  • Check 3 — Headline vs. the story underneath: starts at 100; −35 if half or more of the headline’s content words never appear in the body (−15 if 30% or more); −25 if the most fact-dense sentence sits in the second half of the article; −10 per loaded word in the headline itself.
  • Check 4 — Corrections: BLANK for every outlet. Corrections cannot be scored from a one-day article sample. Scoring it needs the same stories re-fetched after 48 hours to see whether errors were corrected, how fast, and where the correction ran — plus an audit of the outlet’s corrections page. Left blank, not guessed.
  • Check 5 — Anonymous sources: 100 − 12 × the number of anonymous-sourcing phrases in the article (floor 0). Phrases: “critics say”, “sources say”, “many believe”, “some say”, “experts say”, “observers say”, “analysts say”, “it is said”, “reportedly”, “people familiar with”, “according to sources”, “insiders say”, “opponents say”, “supporters say”.

Classification — the rule

The classification is derived from the mean of the four scored checks (Check 4 excluded, because it is unscored for everyone). The mean itself is not displayed — there is no combined score on this board. The rule was fixed before any article was scored, and it applies identically to every outlet:

  • Straight reporting — mean 85 or higher, and no single check below 60.
  • Mostly straight — mean 70 or higher, and no single check below 45.
  • Mixed — spin present — mean 55 or higher.
  • Narrative over news — mean below 55.
  • Unrated — fewer than 3 full-text articles scored, or no retrievable articles at all.

A note on the words. These checks measure wording — whether quotes carry names, whether the headline matches the story, whether opinion is labeled, whether claims rest on unnamed sources — not whether the underlying facts are false. A tier labeled “mostly fake news” would claim more than the checks test. So the tiers say what the checks actually measure: straight reporting versus spin and misleading framing. Whether to use the “fake news” wording is Allan’s call at review.

Honest limits

  • One day, five outlets. 121 articles, one Monday, no story-cycle filter this time — the whole day’s straight-news output as far as it could be retrieved. Outlets may write differently on other days and other subjects. No statistical claims should be made from these means.
  • Keyword heuristics, not comprehension. The scorer matches exact words. The loaded-word list matches substrings — “corruption” trips the watch-list word “corrupt” — and it counts words inside direct quotes and in neutral contexts. Observed in this run: “corruption” tripping “corrupt” (AP russian-newspaper-closes, CNN centrist-dems-warn, MS NOW dems-subpoenas); “admitted” counting as loaded in straightforward trial/reporting contexts (CNN bhatt-trial, nvidia-huang-probe, Fox trump-third-impeachment). Headline words often appear paraphrased in the body, which the tool cannot detect.
  • The quote check counts form, not substance. It rewards quote volume and “said [name]” phrasing, misses the “[name] said” word order, and cannot tell a meaningful quote from a decorative one. A zero-quote brief caps at 25 — a format choice as much as a reporting choice. Short briefs score differently; the 143-word CNN turkey-steel-blast brief is the extreme case.
  • The anonymous-sourcing check uses a fixed phrase list. Other unnamed-sourcing phrasings (e.g. “a senior official said”) are not caught. Only 9 of 121 articles scored below 100 here — that says none used the listed phrases, not that no article relied on unnamed sources in any form.
  • Syndication and provenance. AP and ABC texts were read on syndication partners because their own domains block fetching; minor syndication edits are possible. AP provenance was the main quality risk in this run: 17 candidates were dropped as not AP copy, and 10 AP stories were scored via ABC’s wireStory feed (verbatim AP wire). One AP article carries a Nov 10 republication date on its URL but is the Nov 9 wire copy. Two AP wireStory articles have no captured headline in the dataset (france-iran-fridge-hack, germany-female-president); their receipts rows show the story key instead of a headline.
  • Live pages are scored as single articles. Two rolling live-news pages are in the scored set (CNN, 6,014 words; Fox, 4,249 words). Whether a live page should count as one article, be sampled as its individual updates, or be excluded is still an open call.
  • Analysis-labeled pieces are in the sample. MS NOW dems-target-vouchers is labeled “News Analysis” and was scored as news under the literal rule; ABC south-in-play is analysis-flavoured but unlabeled and was also scored. Whether analysis-labeled pieces belong in the news sample is still an open call.
  • Stories morph the same day. CNN headlines were rewritten between discovery and fetch, and a Fox Business URL from the morning feed now serves a different Nov 9 story than the one it pointed to in the morning. This is the version-tracking phenomenon the Integrity score is meant to catch — observed here, not yet scored.
  • More than one way in. Articles were gathered by Google News RSS discovery, outlets’ own RSS feeds, syndication partners, and Bing News RSS as a URL resolver — more than one route, by design, so no single block can shut the board down. Where an outlet could not be read on any route tried, the coverage table flags it rather than pretending it was scored: if nothing gets out, there is no news outlet to score.
  • Labeling audits are reference only now — and uneven. AP’s is policy-documented; CNN’s and Fox’s come from fetched pages; ABC’s is provisional (site blocked); MS NOW’s is null/unverified. The Nov 10 live-site check found MS NOW has a labeled Opinion lane, CNN has no opinion lane, and ABC has no labeling at all — all three audits may need revisiting. They no longer feed Check 1; they feed the future Integrity score.
  • Corrections (Check 4) are unscored for every outlet. No outlet in this sample has a standalone public corrections page; all correct inside individual articles or on air.
  • No bylines are reported. Scores are by outlet only.
  • Do not mix these numbers with the October backtest or the v2 preview. The Oct 19 pilot (AP 88.6, CNN 85.9, Fox 82.2, NYT 75.8, Reuters 70.6) used a different outlet set, four different axes, and a 50-article sample. The v2 preview (12 articles, Nov 4–8) used the old blended Check 1 and a three-story sample. These are different scores from a different sample and a changed method.
