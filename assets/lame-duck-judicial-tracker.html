The three to watch

Three nominees are stuck, and the reason in each case is a fellow Republican. These are the cases that show how the lame duck actually works.

Zach West — Eastern District of Oklahoma BLOCKED IN COMMITTEE

Who he is: A lawyer in the Oklahoma Attorney General’s office, formerly the state’s solicitor general. Nominated September 14, 2026.

Who is holding him: Senator Alan Armstrong of Oklahoma. Armstrong first returned a favorable blue slip for West, then withdrew it. His stated reason: just purely qualification, versus the other candidates that we’ve suggested. Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina, on the Judiciary Committee, backed Armstrong — and with the committee split 12–10, one Republican defection stops a nominee.

Where it stands: The committee vote on West has been postponed twice, most recently on November 17, with no reason given. The White House would rather West and Trevor Pemberton, the other Oklahoma nominee, move together — so both are stuck.

What could change it: Armstrong leaves the Senate on November 30. Senator-elect Kevin Hern, who succeeds him, has said he would support West — and Tillis has said he would drop his opposition if Hern wants West advanced.

Sources: Reuters, November 15 and 17, 2026; Bloomberg Law, November 17, 2026; Senate Judiciary Committee agenda and releases, November 17, 2026.

T. Elliot Gaiser — Northern District of Texas (Dallas) BLOCKED BEFORE A HEARING

Who he is: Head of the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel. Nominated for a federal bench in Dallas.

Who is holding him: Senator John Cornyn of Texas is withholding the blue slip Gaiser needs for a hearing. Without it, Gaiser cannot appear at the committee hearing scheduled for November 18.

Where it stands: No hearing, no committee vote, no floor vote — the nomination cannot move until Cornyn signs off or the chairman moves it without him.

Sources: Reuters, November 15, 2026; Senate Judiciary Committee hearing schedule, November 18, 2026.

Lee Rudofsky → J. Cody Hiland — Eighth Circuit WITHDRAWN, REPLACED

What happened: The White House withdrew Rudofsky’s nomination to the Eighth Circuit on November 10, and replaced him with Arkansas Supreme Court Justice J. Cody Hiland.

Why: Senators John Boozman and Tom Cotton of Arkansas opposed Rudofsky. Tillis and Cornyn had also privately told the chairman they would vote against him. Rudofsky’s own words: without the support of my home-state senators, confirmation is simply not a practical possibility in today’s Senate.

Where it stands: Hiland is now the nominee for the Eighth Circuit seat. No hearing has been scheduled yet.

Sources: Reuters, November 15, 2026; Bloomberg Law, November 17, 2026.

How the blue slip works

The blue slip is not in the Constitution. It is a custom of the Senate Judiciary Committee.

When a president nominates someone for a district court (trial court) seat, the committee asks both senators from that state whether they approve. If one withholds approval — the blue slip — the nominee usually does not get a hearing. For appeals-court nominees, the committee can move ahead without it.

Senators say it protects their say in who judges their state. Critics say it lets one senator block a nominee over a list or a consultation dispute. Either way, it is a committee custom, not a law — how strictly it is applied is the chairman’s call. And there is no filibuster for judicial nominees anymore, so a single senator’s hold on the committee is often all it takes.
