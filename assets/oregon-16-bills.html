Oregon Scorecard — 16 Bills: Pros, Cons, Poison-Pill / Bundling, Factuality Grades

Final — approved for publishing Oct. 30, 2026 by Allan Plagmann. Oregon only. A factuality grade does not change a scorecard grade or a measure yes/no. Factuality scale, used only: Verified — matches an official source as stated; Mostly accurate — core claim true, detail overstated or a limit left out; Misleading; Unverified — no source found in this research, not “false”; False. Opinions are not graded. SB 5516 remains secondary-sourced only, flagged as such — do not upgrade it to Verified to OLIS. “Bundled” means a voter could reasonably vote no because of one distinct part even if they supported another part.

1. SB 554 (2021) — Firearm storage; Capitol and airport restrictions

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS official summary, B-Engrossed): It requires a firearm not under a person's direct control to be secured with a trigger or cable lock, in a locked container, or in a gun room, and it restricts firearm possession by concealed-handgun licensees in the Capitol, large airport terminals, and other public buildings that adopt such a policy.

Source for this sentence: OLIS, SB 554 B-Engrossed summary, 2021 Regular Session.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED.

Distinct subjects in the bill, all firearms-related but separable:

1. Home/vehicle firearm storage, transfer storage, and lost/stolen reporting (report within 72 hours), with negligence-per-se liability if an unsecured firearm causes injury.

2. Public-building possession: expanded definition of "public building," removal of the concealed-handgun affirmative defense in state buildings, and Capitol / airport-terminal (over 1 million boardings/year) restrictions.

3. Authority for school districts, colleges, universities, and OHSU boards to limit or preclude licensed carry on their grounds.

4. Concealed-handgun license fee increases (OLIS staff summary: initial $50 → $100, renewal $50 → $75).

A voter could support storage rules and oppose the Capitol/airport carry ban, or vice versa, and still have to vote once on the whole bill.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "The bill requires secure storage when a firearm is not under direct control, with a higher penalty if a minor obtains an unsecured firearm." — Verified. OLIS fiscal / B-Engrossed summary: $500 maximum fine generally, $2,000 maximum if a minor obtains it as a result.
  • Supporters' claim that secure storage will reduce child access or gun deaths: Unverified in this research — no outcome study was found in the sources checked for this draft.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill removes the concealed-handgun affirmative defense in state buildings and expands where licensed carriers may not carry." — Verified. OLIS B-Engrossed summary, as above.
  • Critics' claim that the bill "bans all guns in schools, airports, and the Capitol for everyone": Misleading. The text as summarized applies to concealed-handgun licensees' affirmative defense and to possession in specified buildings, with exceptions (for example, unloaded firearms in locked containers for airport transport under federal law), not a blanket ban on all lawful possession by all persons in all circumstances.

Everyday-life effect: If you own a firearm, you must lock it when it is not on you or under your direct control, and you may not carry it into the Capitol or a posted large-airport terminal even with a concealed-handgun license.

2. SB 5514 (2021) — $9.3 billion K-12 State School Fund

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS official budget report): It appropriates $9.3 billion for the 2021–23 State School Fund, the state's share of K-12 school formula funding.

Source: OLIS, SB 5514 Budget Report / Measure Summary, Joint Committee on Ways and Means.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: SINGLE-SUBJECT (appropriation).

One subject: the State School Fund appropriation. The fund-source breakdown (General Fund $8,016,044,524; Lottery Funds; marijuana taxes; Fund for Student Success; Education Stability Fund transfer) is how the $9.3 billion is paid for, not a separate subject. A related Education Stability Fund transfer was in a separate bill (SB 226, per House Democrats' May 2021 release), not in SB 5514 itself.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "The $9.3 billion was a 3.3% increase over the 2019–21 State School Fund, and was described at passage as a record." — Verified. OLIS budget report: $9.0 billion in 2019–21 → $9.3 billion in 2021–23, +3.3%; House Democrats' June 3, 2021 release: "record $9.3 billion."
  • Supporters' claim that $9.3 billion "fully funds" schools with no cuts: Misleading. OSBA/COSA's 2021 Legislative Report, cited in this research, said education advocates calculated $9.6 billion would be needed to avoid cuts at most districts. Whether any given district cut programs is district-specific and was not verified here.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill appropriates $9.3 billion in state funds for K-12 schools." — Verified. OLIS budget report, as above. Whether that level of spending is too high is opinion, no grade.
  • Critics' claim that the bill "raises taxes by $9.3 billion": Misleading. The bill appropriates funds from existing General Fund, Lottery, and other sources as listed in the OLIS report; the bill text summarized here does not itself create a new $9.3 billion tax.

Everyday-life effect: It sets how much state money flows to local school districts for the 2021–23 biennium, which affects school staffing and programs in a child's district, not a direct charge on an individual household bill.

3. HB 2005 (2023) — Unserialized and home-built firearms

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS staff measure summary, House Judiciary): It bans knowingly possessing, selling, transferring, or importing unserialized firearms and unfinished frames or receivers, and bans undetectable firearms, with exceptions including antique firearms, pre-October 22, 1968 firearms, and firearms rendered permanently inoperable.

Source: OLIS, HB 2005 Staff Measure Summary, 2023 Regular Session; ORS 166.266 as summarized in secondary legal summaries.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED (related firearms subjects).

Distinct subjects:

1. Unserialized ("ghost gun") firearms and unfinished frames/receivers — definitions, serialization, and tiered penalties (Class B violation, then misdemeanor/felony on repeat convictions, per OLIS summary).

2. Undetectable firearms — firearms not detectable by walk-through metal detectors or X-ray imaging.

3. History note, RESOLVED Oct 30: House Republicans' June 2023 newsletter called HB 2005 an "omnibus gun control bill," and Ceasefire Oregon noted content from HB 2006 (age-21 purchase/possession) was added to HB 2005B and later removed. Ceasefire lists age-21 under "removed from HB 2005," and InvestigateWest (June 22, 2023) confirms those provisions "were scrapped as part of a deal to bring Senate Republicans back to work." Final law is unserialized / undetectable firearms only — do NOT claim HB 2005 raised the purchase age.

A voter could support banning undetectable firearms and oppose criminalizing home-built unserialized firearms, or vice versa, and still have to vote once.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "The possession ban took effect September 1, 2024, after the sale/transfer/import ban in July 2023, giving owners time to serialize or dispose of unserialized firearms." — Verified. LegalClarity / ORS 166.266 summary, and House Republicans' June 2023 newsletter (September 1, 2024 serialize deadline).
  • Supporters' claim that the ban "will reduce gun crime": Unverified in this research — no Oregon outcome study was found in the sources checked.
  • Critics' factual claim: "A first offense for possessing an unserialized firearm is a Class B violation, with up to $1,000 fine, escalating on repeat offenses." — Mostly accurate. OLIS summary gives the tiered structure (Class B violation, Class A misdemeanor on a prior conviction, Class B felony on two or more priors); the $1,000 figure is from secondary legal summaries of ORS 166.266, not from the OLIS summary excerpt checked here.
  • Critics' opinion that the bill "makes law-abiding citizens criminals overnight": opinion, no grade — the law as summarized includes a delayed possession effective date and exceptions, which is a factual limit on that framing.

Everyday-life effect: If you own a home-built or otherwise unserialized firearm, you must get it serialized by a federally licensed dealer or dispose of it; buying through a licensed dealer is unaffected because those firearms already carry serial numbers.

4. HB 5015 (2023) — $10.2 billion K-12 State School Fund

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS / Legislative Fiscal Office): It appropriates $10.2 billion for the 2023–25 State School Fund, the primary state funding source for school districts and education service districts serving just over 552,000 Oregon students.

Source: OLIS, HB 5015 fiscal / committee documents, May 30, 2023; OSBA, June 26, 2023.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: SINGLE-SUBJECT (appropriation).

One subject: the State School Fund appropriation. The $15.3 billion total formula figure cited in House/Senate releases is the State School Fund ($10.2 billion) plus local revenues (about $5.1 billion, mostly property taxes) — local revenue is not appropriated by this bill.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "The $10.2 billion was a $900 million, 9.7% increase over the 2021–23 $9.3 billion, and $700 million above current service level." — Verified. OLIS fiscal document: total funds $9.3 billion → $10.2 billion, +$900 million / 9.7%; OEA summary: "$700 million above current service level."
  • Supporters' claim that $10.2 billion "fully funds" schools: Misleading if stated without qualification. OSBA, June 26, 2023: education advocates calculated $10.3 billion was needed for most districts to avoid shortfalls, and the Quality Education Commission figure cited by OSBA was $11.9 billion. Some districts still faced shortfalls at $10.2 billion, per OSBA.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill appropriates $10.2 billion in state funds." — Verified. OLIS fiscal document, as above.
  • Critics' claim that this is "a record that proves spending is out of control": opinion on "out of control," no grade; the "record" part is Verified as a nominal record, with OSBA's qualification that inflation means the fund sets a nominal record almost every biennium.

Everyday-life effect: It sets state school funding for 2023–25, which affects a local school district's budget, staffing, and programs, not a direct per-household charge.

5. SB 243 (2025) — Rapid-fire activators; public-building carry; Measure 114 timing

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS / Oregon Counties summary of the enacted law): It bans "rapid-fire activators" such as bump stocks, lets cities, counties, and special districts bar concealed-handgun license holders from carrying in public buildings used for official meetings, and sets Measure 114 implementation timing.

Source: Association of Oregon Counties, September 26, 2025 effective-date summary; Ceasefire Oregon, SB 243 status page.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED. This is the clearest poison-pill example in the 16.

Three distinct components, confirmed by the Association of Oregon Counties:

1. Rapid-fire activator ban — possession is a Class A misdemeanor; manufacture/transport is a Class B felony (up to 10 years, per secondary summaries of the law).

2. Local public-building carry bans — cities, counties, and special districts (special districts added by this bill) may bar licensed carry in buildings used for official meetings, with signage and website posting required.

3. Measure 114 implementation — SB 243 set Measure 114 provisions to go into effect March 15, 2026 (later changed to January 1, 2028 by HB 4145, see entry 16).

Amendment history also shows bundling pressure: a 72-hour waiting period was added by amendment and removed before passage (Firearm Safety Team summary; Ceasefire Oregon notes the removal as a concession). A voter could support the activator ban and oppose the local carry bans or the Measure 114 timing, or vice versa, and still have to vote once.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "Rapid-fire activators, including bump stocks, binary triggers, and forced-reset triggers, are now illegal to possess, manufacture, sell, or transport in Oregon, effective September 26, 2025." — Verified. Ceasefire Oregon SB 243 page; Oregon Counties effective-date summary; PDX Arsenal summary of penalties.
  • Supporters' claim that these devices "essentially turn semi-automatic firearms into fully automatic weapons": Mostly accurate as a functional description, not a legal classification. The devices increase rate of fire; the U.S. Supreme Court in 2024 held bump stocks do not meet the federal definition of a machine gun, which is why Oregon enacted a state-level ban — that federal/state distinction is a factual limit on the claim.
  • Critics' factual claim: "A city, county, or special district may now bar a valid concealed-handgun license holder from a posted public meeting building, with a Class A misdemeanor penalty." — Verified. Firearm Safety Team / PDX Arsenal summaries of ORS 166.377 as amended by SB 243.
  • Critics' claim that SB 243 "raised the purchase age for semiautomatic rifles to 21": False for the final enrolled bill — RESOLVED Oct 30. Age-21 and a 72-hour wait were in the -1 amendment (Mar. 27, 2025), but both were stripped before passage (Ceasefire/Indivisible: "The age limitation in SB 243 was stripped away before the bill passed"; HD-15 update, May 17, 2025, same). Do not repeat the age-21 claim as final law; it was true of an earlier amendment version only.

Everyday-life effect: If you own a bump stock or similar activator, possession is now a crime in Oregon; if you carry with a license, you must check for posted signs at city, county, and special-district meeting buildings before entering.

6. HB 3054 (2025) — Rent control in manufactured-home parks and marinas

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (Oregon Housing and Community Services FAQ, citing ORS 90.324 as amended): For parks and marinas with more than 30 spaces, it caps annual rent increases at 6% beginning in 2026, with a different rule (10%, or 7% plus CPI, whichever is less) for facilities with 30 or fewer spaces.

Source: OHCS, Manufactured & Marina Communities FAQ; Oregon Real Estate Agency, 2025 Legislative Round-Up.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED (related housing provisions).

Distinct provisions in the bill as summarized by OLIS staff and the Real Estate Agency:

1. Rent cap (6% for larger facilities, as above).

2. Sale/transfer conditions: prohibits landlords from requiring interior inspections or aesthetic/cosmetic improvements as a condition of a sale, and limits rent for a new owner.

3. Tenant-vote exception: up to a 12% increase, once every five years, for a significant infrastructure project, if approved by a written vote of 51% of occupied spaces, in place of a regular increase.

4. Early-version provision, removed by amendment: a state study of water and sewer systems in these communities, due September 15, 2026 (OLIS -2 staff summary, amendment note).

A voter could support the rent cap and oppose the sale-condition rules, or vice versa, and still have to vote once.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "For larger parks and marinas, the maximum annual increase is 6% beginning in 2026." — Verified. OHCS FAQ; Real Estate Agency Round-Up.
  • Supporters' claim that the cap "protects residents from being priced out": opinion / outcome claim, Unverified in this research — no Oregon outcome study on displacement after HB 3054 was found in the sources checked.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill caps rent increases and prohibits landlords from requiring inspections or cosmetic improvements as a sale condition." — Verified. Real Estate Agency Round-Up; OLIS -2 staff summary.
  • Critics' claim that rent control "always reduces housing quality and supply": Unverified as a universal claim in this research — it is a general economic argument, not a verified Oregon-specific outcome for HB 3054.

Everyday-life effect: If you rent a space in a manufactured-home park or marina with more than 30 spaces, your annual space-rent increase is capped at 6%; if you sell your manufactured home in place, the landlord may not require an inspection or cosmetic upgrades as a sale condition.

7. HB 2385 (2025) — 340B prescription-drug program mandates

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS staff measure summary, Senate Health Care): It makes it an unlawful trade practice for a drug manufacturer to deny, restrict, prohibit, or otherwise interfere with a pharmacy's ability to acquire or dispense 340B-discounted drugs for a covered entity, unless federal HHS prohibits that acquisition.

Source: OLIS, HB 2385 A Staff Measure Summary, Senate Committee on Health Care.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED.

Distinct provisions in the B-Engrossed digest (OLIS):

1. Manufacturer interference ban (above), including a ban on requiring utilization-review data as a condition of acquiring a 340B drug, unless HHS requires it.

2. Rules for how covered entities must use 340B drug profits.

3. Creation of a 340B Drug Fund.

4. A reporting requirement to the Department of Consumer and Business Services about 340B drugs.

A voter could support the anti-interference rule and oppose the profit-use / fund / reporting rules, or vice versa, and still have to vote once.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "Since 2020, manufacturers had restricted 340B discounts at contract pharmacies, and OPCA surveyed members and estimated $10 million in lost savings for Oregon FQHCs in 2024." — Mostly accurate. The $10 million figure is OPCA's survey estimate, reported by OPCA (PRLog, June 12, 2025), not an audited state figure — the estimate's source is the limit on the claim.
  • Supporters' factual claim: "HB 2385 forbids manufacturers from restricting where 340B medications can be dispensed, and empowers the Oregon Board of Pharmacy to fine non-compliant manufacturers." — Verified as to the ban; the enforcement provision is stated in OPCA's summary and in the OLIS summary's unlawful-trade-practice framing. The exact fine amounts were Unverified in this research.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill uses state power to dictate terms between drug makers, pharmacies, and benefit managers." — Verified as a description of the mechanism (unlawful trade practice for manufacturer interference, per OLIS). Whether that is constitutionally improper is opinion / legal argument, no grade.
  • Critics' claim that the bill "will raise drug prices for everyone": Unverified in this research — no Oregon price-outcome study for HB 2385 was found in the sources checked.

Everyday-life effect: If you get prescriptions through a community health center or other safety-net provider, the bill is intended to keep discounted 340B drugs available at your local contract pharmacy, rather than only at a manufacturer-approved location; it does not set your individual prescription price.

9. SB 551 (2025) — Plastic checkout bags only (utensils and lodging rules removed before passage)

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS staff measure summary, as introduced; final version narrowed — see flag): As introduced, it would prohibit retailers and restaurants from providing reusable plastic checkout bags, prohibit food providers from providing single-use plastic utensils or condiment packaging unless requested, and prohibit lodging establishments from providing small single-use plastic toiletry containers unless requested.

Source: OLIS, SB 551 Staff Measure Summary, Senate Energy and Environment.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED AS INTRODUCED, NARROWED AS ENACTED.

Distinct subjects as introduced:

1. Checkout bags (reusable plastic/fabric).

2. Single-use plastic utensils and condiment packaging (food and beverage providers / convenience stores, on-request only).

3. Small plastic toiletry containers in lodging establishments (on-request only), plus DEQ inspection authority for that provision.

Final-version note — RESOLVED Oct 30, enrolled-text check: House -A4 (OLIS staff summary) removed the utensil/condiment prohibition and the lodging prohibition, and Oceana (May 2025) confirms the House removed both sections. Final law is checkout bags only, Jan. 1, 2027. Do NOT publish the utensil (July 1, 2026) or lodging (Jan. 1, 2027 / Jan. 1, 2028) dates as final-law dates. This is a textbook poison-pill / narrowing history: a voter who supported the bag ban but opposed the utensil/lodging rules, or vice versa, faced a different bill at different stages.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "From January 1, 2027, retailers and restaurants may not provide reusable plastic or fabric checkout bags; retailers may provide recycled-paper bags for at least five cents, restaurants may provide them free, with WIC/EBT exceptions." — Verified for the bag-ban portion. The utensil/lodging provisions are False as final law — they were in earlier versions only and were removed by House -A4 (see flag above).
  • Supporters' claim that the 2019 bag ban "reduced thin plastic bag use and shoreline litter": Mostly accurate, with a limit. DEQ reports cited in a March 2026 review show a shift away from thin single-use bags and a 2025 peer-reviewed study found 25–47% fewer plastic bags at shoreline cleanups in ban jurisdictions; comprehensive Oregon statewide waste-generation data through 2024 had not been published as of March 28, 2026, so a definitive net-waste claim is Unverified.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill does not ban shoppers from bringing their own reusable bags." — Verified. The rules summarized here concern bags businesses provide, not bags shoppers bring.
  • Critics' claim that the ban "will increase overall plastic waste because people buy thicker trash bags": Unverified for Oregon in net tonnage terms in this research — substitution effects are noted as real concerns in the March 2026 review, but not quantified for Oregon.

Everyday-life effect: From January 1, 2027, a store or restaurant will not hand you a plastic or fabric reusable checkout bag — you will need to bring your own bag or take a recycled-paper bag (at least five cents at a retailer, free at a restaurant, with WIC/EBT exceptions at retailers).

10. SB 827 (2025) — Expanded solar and energy-storage rebates

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (Oregon Department of Energy, 2025 Legislative Report): It modifies the Oregon Solar + Storage Rebate Program to allow rebates for energy-storage systems paired with previously installed solar systems, effective January 1, 2026.

Source: ODOE, 2025 Legislative Report, SB 827 section; ODOE Solar + Storage Rebate Rulemaking page.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: SINGLE-SUBJECT (one program).

One subject: the Solar + Storage Rebate Program. Program mechanics — storage-only rebates capped at no more than 25% of rebate funds in a calendar year, consumer-protection rulemaking, and a change in the annual report date (to September 15 for the previous fiscal year) — are administration of that one program, not separate subjects.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "SB 827 creates a new rebate for battery storage paired at installation with previously purchased and installed solar systems, and ODOE filed final rules April 28, 2026, effective April 29, 2026." — Verified. ODOE Rulemaking page, as above.
  • Supporters' factual claim: "For low- and moderate-income customers, rebates can cover up to 60% of net cost (or $5,000 for solar, $2,500 for storage), versus 40% for other residential customers, with 25% of funds reserved for low-income customers." — Mostly accurate. Those figures are from BillTrack50's summary of the bill as introduced/amended; ODOE's legislative report confirms the storage-only expansion and the 25% cap, but the exact percentage caps were not re-verified against the enrolled text in this research.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill expands taxpayer-funded rebates for private solar/battery purchases." — Verified as a description of the mechanism (rebates administered by ODOE). Whether that "distorts the free market" is opinion, no grade.
  • Critics' claim that the program is "currently unfunded, so the expansion has no effect": Mostly accurate at the time of ODOE's report. ODOE's 2025 Legislative Report states: "The Oregon Solar + Storage Rebate Program is currently unfunded, so these changes would go into practice only if the program is funded in the future." That is a limit on the everyday-life effect, not a contradiction of the rebate law itself.

Everyday-life effect: If you already have solar panels and later add a battery, you may qualify for a storage rebate — but only if the program is funded; as of ODOE's 2025 report it was unfunded, so do not promise a rebate amount to a homeowner today.

11. HB 2530 (2025) — School-bus stop-arm cameras

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS staff measure summary, Senate Education): It sets the framework for education providers, in agreement with a law enforcement agency, to contract with a private entity to install stop-arm cameras on school buses and to issue citations, based on camera evidence reviewed and signed by a police officer, to drivers who pass a bus with its safety lights flashing.

Source: OLIS, HB 2530 A Staff Measure Summary, Senate Committee on Education.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: SINGLE-SUBJECT (one program, multiple mechanics).

One subject: school-bus stop-arm cameras, amending the 2024 law (HB 4147). Mechanics inside that program — private contracting, a rebuttable presumption that warning signs were posted and lights were active/operational, a certificate-of-nonliability process, and distribution of conviction money to the education provider that installed the camera — are all parts of the same camera-citation program, not unrelated subjects. A voter who opposes the revenue-distribution part but supports the safety part still faces a single-program vote, and that limit should be stated plainly.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "A citation based on stop-arm camera evidence requires a police officer to have reviewed the evidence and signed the citation, and a warning sign must be posted on the bus." — Verified. OLIS enrolled text summary / staff summary, as above.
  • Supporters' factual claim, from Bus Patrol testimony materials: "90% of violators who get a ticket from a Bus Patrol program don't illegally pass a school bus a second time." — Unverified in this research — that figure is from a vendor's program materials, not an Oregon state study found in the sources checked. Do not present it as an Oregon outcome.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill creates a rebuttable presumption that signs were posted and lights were active, so a court assumes those facts unless the recipient provides evidence to the contrary, and it directs conviction money to the education provider that installed the camera." — Verified. OLIS staff summary, as above.
  • Critics' opinion that this is "automated government surveillance": opinion framing, no grade — the factual mechanism (camera photographs plate, date, time, location; driver is not photographed, per enrolled text summary) is Verified as a limit on that framing.

Everyday-life effect: If you drive past a stopped school bus with its red lights flashing, a bus camera can photograph your plate and a police officer can mail you a citation, even if no officer was present at the bus.

12. SB 1014 (2025) — Translated party statements in the voters' pamphlet

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS staff measure summary, House Rules; Secretary of State 2025 Synopsis): It requires the Secretary of State to allow translations of a political party's statement of arguments, filed by the original author, to be included in the translated state and county voters' pamphlets on the Secretary of State's and county websites, in one of the five most commonly spoken languages in Oregon other than English.

Source: OLIS, SB 1014 Staff Measure Summary; Oregon Secretary of State, 2025 Synopsis of Oregon Election Laws, ORS 251.170 summary.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: SINGLE-SUBJECT.

One subject: translated party statements in the voters' pamphlet. It amends ORS 251.170 only, applies to pamphlets distributed on or after January 31, 2026, and does not change candidate statements, measure arguments, or ballot language beyond the party-statement translation provision summarized here.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "The bill allows, not requires, a party to file a translation of its own statement, in a top-five non-English language, for online translated pamphlets." — Verified. OLIS staff summary and SOS Synopsis both frame it as allowing party-filed translations, with the author-filing and language conditions.
  • Supporters' claim that this "increases voter participation among non-English speakers": Unverified in this research — no Oregon participation-outcome study for SB 1014 was found in the sources checked.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill requires the Secretary of State to allow translated party statements on the Secretary of State's and county websites." — Verified. OLIS staff summary, as above. The Freedom Index description that the bill "mandates translated political materials" is Misleading if read as a mandate to translate every party statement — the bill as summarized allows party-filed translations, it does not require the state to translate a party's statement itself.
  • Critics' opinion that multilingual pamphlets "undermine a shared civic culture": opinion, no grade.

Everyday-life effect: If you read the voters' pamphlet online in one of Oregon's five most commonly spoken non-English languages, a political party's statement may now appear in that language, if the party filed a translation; the English pamphlet itself is unchanged.

13. HB 2586 (2025) — In-state tuition for asylum seekers

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS Enrolled HB 2586-A, RESOLVED Oct 30): It exempts an asylum seeker who is a student at an Oregon public university or OHSU from nonresident tuition and fees, subject to enrolled conditions — Oregon school attendance during each of the 3 years before diploma/leaving school, US school attendance during each of the 5 years before diploma/leaving school, an Oregon secondary diploma, and, for the OHSU section, not having previously established residence in another US state/territory other than Oregon — amending ORS 352.287 and 353.123, and makes those groups eligible for scholarships / financial aid consistent with board policies.

Source for this sentence: OLIS, Enrolled HB 2586-A (2025).

Poison-pill / bundling flag: SINGLE-SUBJECT (enrolled text, Oct 30).

One subject: nonresident tuition exemption + financial-aid eligibility for asylum seekers (and the same groups already in ORS 352.287 / 353.123 — refugees, COFA islanders, special immigrant visa holders). No unrelated subject was found in the enrolled text checked.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "The bill allows asylum seekers who are students at Oregon public universities or OHSU to pay in-state rates instead of nonresident rates, subject to enrolled school-attendance and residency conditions." — Verified to enrolled text (Oct 30). The generic "intent to pursue lawful status" phrasing is replaced by the enrolled conditions in the What-it-does sentence above; a pending application for asylum counts in the enrolled definitions.
  • Supporters' claim about how many students this affects or how much it saves them: Unverified in this research — no enrollment or savings figure was found in the sources checked.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill grants in-state tuition to asylum seekers at taxpayer-funded public universities and OHSU." — Verified as a description of the mechanism (public universities / OHSU, in-state rate). Whether that is "special treatment" or "unfair to citizens" is opinion, no grade.
  • Critics' claim that recipients "entered the country illegally": Misleading as a blanket description of asylum seekers — asylum seekers, by definition, have filed or intend to file an asylum claim under federal law, which is a lawful process; the enrolled conditions are school-attendance, diploma, and residency conditions, and a pending application for asylum counts in the enrolled definitions. Individual immigration histories vary and were not verified here.

Everyday-life effect: If you are an asylum-seeking student at an Oregon public university or OHSU and you meet the enrolled school-attendance, diploma, and residency conditions, your tuition bill drops from the nonresident rate to the in-state rate; if you are not in that group, the bill does not change your tuition rate directly.

14. HB 3792 (2025) — Doubled energy-bill surcharge for low-income aid

Scorecard position: NO

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS A-Engrossed summary): It increases from $20 million to $40 million the minimum amount to be collected from electric-company customers for low-income electric bill payment and crisis assistance, and increases from $500 to $1,000 per month per customer site the maximum amount a customer may be required to pay for that assistance.

Source: OLIS, HB 3792 A-Engrossed summary.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED MECHANICS WITHIN ONE PROGRAM, PLUS A SEPARATE STUDY.

Distinct provisions:

1. Doubling the minimum annual collection ($20 million → $40 million) for the Oregon Energy Assistance Program / Low-Income Electric Bill Payment Assistance Fund.

2. Doubling the per-site monthly cap ($500 → $1,000).

3. PUC biennial reassessment of need, with a report to the Legislative Assembly if the PUC increases the amount by more than 2.5%.

4. A separate PUC study of consumer protection for utility ratepayers, with findings due to interim committees by September 15, 2026 (OLIS A-Engrossed summary).

Items 1–3 are one program's funding mechanics; item 4 is a separate study task. A voter could support the assistance funding and oppose the higher per-site cap, or vice versa, and still have to vote once.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "HB 3792 doubles yearly funding for energy bill help from $20 million to $40 million for low-income and senior households to prevent shut-offs, and doubles the per-site cap from $500 to $1,000 per month." — Verified. Governor's Office signing release, August 2025; OLIS A-Engrossed summary, as above.
  • Supporters' factual claim, from OHCS: "Since launching in 1999, the Oregon Energy Assistance Program has assisted more than 85,000 customers, helping 92% of participants avoid shut-offs." — Mostly accurate. That figure is OHCS's program history as reported August 27, 2025; it describes the pre-existing program, not an outcome of HB 3792 itself.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill doubles the minimum amount collected from electric customers for low-income assistance." — Verified. OLIS A-Engrossed summary, as above. The exact amount any individual household pays depends on usage and utility, and was not stated in the sources checked — do not invent a per-household dollar figure.
  • Critics' opinion that this "redistributes wealth through utility bills": opinion, no grade.

Everyday-life effect: Every electric customer's bill carries a surcharge for low-income bill assistance, with the statewide minimum collection doubled to $40 million a year — your own bill's surcharge amount depends on your utility and usage, and low-income households at risk of shut-off may receive more assistance because the program's minimum collection doubled to $40 million a year; the $1,000 figure is the cap on what a customer site may be charged in surcharges, not an assistance amount.

15. SB 1587 (2026) — Limits on sharing data for immigration enforcement

Scorecard position: YES

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS Open Government Impact Statement, SB 1587-B): It prohibits a public body from disclosing personally identifiable information to a data broker unless the broker first provides a written attestation that the information will not be sold or transferred to any entity that will use it to enforce federal immigration law.

Source: OLIS, SB 1587-B Open Government Impact Statement, February 27, 2026; Oregon State Bar, 2026 Legislation Highlights.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: SINGLE-SUBJECT.

One subject: public-body disclosure to data brokers, with attestation. Exceptions are limits on that one subject, not separate subjects: disclosure required by public-records law or court order, disclosure under the same terms as information available to the general public, and a public body's right to withhold disclosure if it reasonably believes the attestation contains material misrepresentations, falsehoods, or omissions.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "The bill prohibits public bodies from disclosing personally identifiable information to data brokers without an accepted attestation, and took effect June 5, 2026." — Verified. OLIS impact statement; OS Bar Highlights (effective June 5, 2026).
  • Supporters' factual claim: "The original bill was broader, and was narrowed in session to focus on state-agency data handled by brokers." — Verified. Oregon Capital Chronicle, July 2, 2026: lead author Sen. Wlnsvey Campos narrowed the approach, with a commitment to broader privacy legislation in 2027.
  • Critics' factual claim: "The bill does not stop a data broker from obtaining the same information if it is already available to the general public under the same terms, or if disclosure is required by court order or public-records law." — Verified. OLIS impact statement exceptions, as above. That is a factual limit on the bill's reach, not an opinion.
  • Critics' claim that the bill "blocks all immigration enforcement in Oregon": False as a description of this bill — the bill as summarized addresses public-body disclosure to data brokers only, with the exceptions above, and does not itself bar federal immigration enforcement or other state/federal cooperation beyond that disclosure rule.

Everyday-life effect: If a state or local agency holds your personal information in a public record, it may not hand that information to a data broker who will not promise in writing not to pass it on for federal immigration enforcement; it does not change what information is already public, or what a court can order disclosed.

16. HB 4145 (2026) — Delays Measure 114 gun law to 2028

Scorecard position: YES

What it does, in one sentence (OLIS staff measure summary, Senate Rules, -A9): It delays the operation of Ballot Measure 114's permit-to-purchase requirement to January 1, 2028 (and large-capacity magazine provisions on the timetable in the enrolled bill), while the Oregon Supreme Court decides Measure 114's constitutionality.

Source: OLIS, HB 4145 A -A9 Staff Measure Summary, Senate Committee on Rules.

Poison-pill / bundling flag: FLAGGED — BUNDLED (multiple Measure 114 changes in one bill).

Distinct changes in the bill as summarized by OLIS:

1. Delay of the permit-to-purchase operative date (from March 15, 2026, to January 1, 2028).

2. Delay / timing changes for large-capacity magazine prohibitions — RESOLVED Oct 30: the Jan. 1, 2027 magazine date in one earlier OLIS summary version does not survive as a separate final date in the -A9 summary. Use Jan. 1, 2028 as the -A9 operative date for Measure 114 permit/magazine provisions, subject to the Supreme Court case.

3. Changes to permit eligibility and exemptions, background-check processes, record retention and reporting, and the proof-of-completion definition, large-capacity magazine affirmative defense, and exceptions. (The original House version's fee increases and processing-time changes were scrapped by Senate -A9 and are NOT final law — see pros/cons below.)

4. Venue: challenges to the measure's legality or constitutionality to be filed in Marion County Circuit Court.

A voter could support delaying the permit requirement and oppose changing permit eligibility, background-check processes, or record rules, or vice versa, and still have to vote once. The bill also expressly states legislative intent not to interfere with the pending Supreme Court proceedings.

Pros and cons:

  • Supporters' factual claim: "Measure 114 was passed by voters in November 2022, has never been enforced, and its permit and magazine provisions are now set to apply January 1, 2028, unless a court decision or later legislation changes that." — Verified. LegalClarity Measure 114 summary; That Oregon Life, September 14, 2026, citing Oregon Laws 2026, chapter 120 (HB 4145).
  • Supporters' factual claim: "A circuit court found Measure 114 unconstitutional in 2023, the Court of Appeals disagreed, and the Oregon Supreme Court heard oral arguments in November 2025 and had not ruled as of early 2026." — Verified. LegalClarity Measure 114 summary, as above.
  • Critics' factual claim: "HB 4145 does not repeal Measure 114; it delays and modifies it, and the requirements cannot be enforced until courts lift the injunction and the January 2028 date arrives." — Verified. LegalClarity HB 4145 section, as above.
  • Critics' claim, from some 2026 testimony, that HB 4145 "hikes permit fees from $65 to $150 and doubles processing time to 60 days": True of the original House version only, False as final law — RESOLVED Oct 30. Those figures are in the original HB 4145-A fiscal impact (Feb. 16, 2026: $65→$150, renewal $50→$110, up to 60 days), but Senate Rules -A9 replaced the entire bill, and That Oregon Life / TheGunMag confirm those fee/time changes "were scrapped in the final version." Do NOT publish $65→$150 or 30→60 days as final-law facts. The -A9 version also modifies proof-of-completion definition, large-capacity magazine affirmative defense, and exceptions — so "only the date changed" is a simplification; the fee/time changes are the parts that were scrapped.

Everyday-life effect: If you buy a firearm in Oregon, you do not need a Measure 114 purchase permit before January 1, 2028, under the current timetable — existing background-check requirements through the Oregon State Police still apply, and the Supreme Court case could still change the outcome.
