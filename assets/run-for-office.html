So You Want to Run for Office?

You want to run. The consultants take ~20% off everything, and the party won't back you unless you use one of their approved firms. This kit is the way around that. Everything here is public information — from the Secretary of State's own manuals and from FEC filings. It just puts it in one place, in plain English. It is not legal advice: every section points at the official source so you can verify it there.

Who this is for: a first-time challenger in Oregon, any party or none, for any office — US House, state legislature, statewide, county, city.

A. Get on the ballot

Filing costs almost nothing. The fee is $25 to $150 — or signatures instead, which cost time, not money.

Source: Oregon Secretary of State, State Candidate Manual Rev. 11/2025, Filing Requirements for Partisan Office (pp. 9–10); ORS 249.031, 249.068, 249.740. County and city offices file with the county clerk or city elections official under a separate manual, and city fees are set by charter or ordinance.

The rules that catch people

  • The window. File no sooner than the 250th day and no later than the 70th day before the election (ORS 249.037). Everything must be actually received by 5:00 PM on the deadline — a postmark does not count.
  • Incumbents file a week early. An incumbent seeking re-election to the same office must file at least seven days before the deadline for everyone else (HB 4024, 2024).
  • The 180-day rule. A major-party candidate must have been registered with that party for at least 180 days before the filing deadline. A nonaffiliated candidate must have been registered nonaffiliated for at least 180 days before the certificate-of-nomination deadline.
  • Signatures take time. Get them in about two weeks before the deadline — that is the manual's "guaranteed verification" date. Later ones might not be verified in time, and then you pay the fee instead.
  • The prospective petition. You can file the fee and gather signatures at the same time. If the signatures verify by the deadline, the fee is refunded.
  • One office only. You may file for one lucrative office per election (ORS 249.013).
  • Minor parties nominate by convention, not by primary. Nonaffiliated candidates go by Individual Electors petition, or by an Assembly of Electors — a one-day convention, in one place, during a single 12-hour period, supervised by Elections Division staff, with notice published in at least three newspapers in the district.

B. The money — what it actually costs

The rules

  • Within 3 business days of your first contribution or first expenditure, file a Statement of Organization (SEL 220) naming your candidate committee — and no later than the filing deadline, whichever comes first. Then report every contribution and expenditure in ORESTAR (state races) or with the FEC (federal races).
  • Open a separate campaign bank account. The committee, not you personally, receives and spends.
  • Your voters' pamphlet statement goes in through ORESTAR too — fee, or a voters' pamphlet petition with the required signatures in lieu of the fee. Get approval before gathering pamphlet-petition signatures.

The real dollars

Getting on the ballot costs $25 to $150. That is the cheap part. What a campaign actually spends is the real number — and it is all public:

  • 2026, through the Oct 14 filing (OR-05): Janelle Bynum raised $4,737,332 and spent $2,606,483. Her challenger David Brock Smith raised $311,174 and spent $266,976. Patti Adair raised $682,239 and spent $559,654; Monique DeSpain raised $921,876 and spent $768,300.
  • 2024, closed cycle (OR-05): Bynum spent $7,479,499 — 77% of it ($5,762,880) went to one media-buying firm. Chavez-DeRemer spent $5,745,404 — her top five firms together took 67%.
  • What firms actually got paid, 2026: Bynum's top firm, Ascend Digital, $217,084 (8% of her spending). Brock Smith's top firm, PT Strategy, $16,983. Adair's top firm, TAG LLC, $76,010. All of it is on the ballot pages now, under "Where the money went."
  • The consultant's cut: the industry-standard media commission is 15% of the ad buy. On a $100,000 ad buy, that is roughly $15,000 to the firm. The commission itself is never itemized on a filing — the campaign reports one sum paid to the firm. So this is what the campaign paid, not what the firm kept.
Gap, stated plainly: state-legislature spending is not in this kit yet. State races report through ORESTAR, which has no bulk download — the figures have to be hand-pulled per race. That pull has not been done.

C. Reach voters without a consultant — the free tools

  • VoterLens itself. The ballot in plain English, the grades, the money trail, the vendor line. Read your opponent's filings the same way a voter can — and send voters the same link.
  • The voters' pamphlet. Every candidate can put a statement in front of every voter in the district. Fee or petition signatures — the same route as the filing fee.
  • oregonvotes.gov / ORESTAR. File candidacy, file the pamphlet statement, report money. It is the state's system, and it is free.
  • fec.gov/data. Every federal filing is public. If your opponent is a federal candidate, their donors and their vendors are already published.
  • Knock doors. The free tools replace the firm's data and the firm's ads, not the walking.

D. The checklist — in order

  1. Pick the office. Check you meet its qualifications and the 180-day registration rule.
  2. Decide: fee or signatures. If signatures, file the prospective petition early — verification takes time, and the fee is refunded if they verify.
  3. File by 5 PM on the deadline (ORESTAR online for a major-party or nonpartisan primary filing; paper SEL 101 / 110 / 114 / 115 for the rest).
  4. Within 3 business days of your first dollar in or out: Statement of Organization (SEL 220) and a separate campaign bank account.
  5. Report every contribution and expenditure — ORESTAR for state races, FEC for federal.
  6. File your voters' pamphlet statement through ORESTAR (fee or petition).
  7. Reach voters: VoterLens is free, the pamphlet reaches every voter, and knock doors.
  8. (Optional) Look for the badge — see below.

E. The no-consultant badge

No fundraising consultants

The filings show who was paid, how much, and for what — never how the fee was structured. So the badge is about purpose: no firm paid for fundraising. Its job is curiosity — a voter sees it, taps it, and goes to check the money. And it points out the newcomer who ran without paying to play, so the next person who wants to run can see it's possible.

The check. Every recipient in the campaign's 2026 FEC filings was checked against the full filing — not just the top five. A candidate passes when no filing row pays a firm for fundraising (including finance consulting, which counts as fundraising). The check runs on the filings as filed, through the Oct 14, 2026 quarterly filing.

Who holds it now. Four candidates pass the full-filing check. All four are under the $25,000 vendor-line floor, so the check covered the full filing in each case:

  • Chris Beck (U.S. House, OR-02 — challenger vs. incumbent Cliff Bentz): 36 rows checked, 100% coverage. Spent $163,849. What he did pay for: printing, postage and assembly ($6,063), a voter list ($5,000), website work ($1,750), lodging ($1,049), FEC compliance and management services ($4,089), credit card merchant fees ($1,953).
  • Barbara J Kahl (U.S. House, OR-01): 35 rows checked, 100% coverage. What she did pay for: signage ($1,095), fuel including fuel for canvassing ($1,004), palm cards ($886).
  • Justin Filip (U.S. House, OR-04): 1 row checked, 100% coverage. What he did pay for: hardware and software ($1,030).
  • David Russ (U.S. House, OR-06): 15 rows checked, 100% coverage. His largest entries are in-kind contributions and forgiven debt, not payments to firms.

See the badge on their ballot cards, and the check itself in each candidate's "Follow the money" block.

One honest limit, stated plainly. The badge check reads the purpose written on each filing row. A generic label can hide fundraising: of the $76,010 Patti Adair's campaign paid TAG LLC, only $23,684 is labeled fundraising fees — $52,326 is labeled digital consulting. Adair fails the check regardless, but the limit is real: the badge proves what the filings say, not what a firm actually did. And the vendor line shows the campaign's own spending, not Super PAC independent expenditures — those are a separate filing, and a future addition.
