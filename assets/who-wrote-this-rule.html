Who Wrote This Rule?

The unelected officials who make the rules — traced, named, and shown in plain English.

← Back to VoterLens

Congress writes the laws. But most of the rules you actually live under were written by people nobody voted for — the heads of federal agencies. This door shows the chain for every rule: who wrote it, what law it claims to come from, whether Congress ever voted on it, what it costs, and what the courts did with it. It shows the chain. It doesn’t editorialize.

One rule per card, same nine fields in the same order. If a field couldn’t be verified, it says Unverified — never filled in. No grades. No verdicts beyond what a court actually ruled. Every claim carries its source. Each card carries a “checked on” date — rules change, and the card says when it was last checked.

The scale: about 3,000 federal final rules a year (2023: 3,018; 2024: 3,248; 2025: 2,441). Of those, 168 were “major” rules in 2024 — each one with an annual effect of $100 million or more. (Source: CEI tallies of National Archives / Office of the Federal Register data. The 2023 major-rule count is held until a direct check of the GAO database settles it — two published figures disagree, so no number is given here.) Every one of those rules was written by an agency, not by Congress. Congress voted on the law the rule claims to come from — sometimes decades ago — but almost never on the rule itself.

How a rule is made (5 steps)

  1. Congress delegates. A statute gives an agency authority to make rules on a subject.
  2. The agency proposes. A Notice of Proposed Rulemaking is published in the Federal Register, citing the legal authority. The APA sets no minimum comment length; in practice it’s typically 30–60 days.
  3. The public comments. Anyone can submit views. The agency must answer the significant ones.
  4. The agency finalises. The final rule is published at least 30 days before it takes effect, then codified in the Code of Federal Regulations.
  5. The courts and Congress can stop it. A court can strike it down. Congress can kill it with the Congressional Review Act. Both are shown on each card below.

The three levers

1. The Congressional Review Act — the delete button, and it works. Congress can pass a joint resolution of disapproval; the Senate can’t filibuster it. If signed, the rule has no force or effect — and no substantially similar rule can be reissued without a new law. 43 rules killed this way (CRS, updated Oct. 1, 2026): 2001: 1; 2017: 15; 2018: 1; 2021: 3; 2025: 22; 2026: 1. In the 118th Congress, 11 disapprovals passed and all 11 were vetoed — 0 became law.

2. The REINS Act — the approve-first lever, never enacted. It would flip the default: a major rule would have no force unless Congress affirmatively approved it within 70 legislative days. The House passed it most recently in June 2023 (H.R. 277, 221–210); it died in the Senate. In the 119th Congress, H.R. 142 and S. 485 are both still only Introduced — no hearing, no floor vote.

3. Loper Bright — the court lever. Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 603 U.S. 369 (2024): the Supreme Court overruled Chevron. Courts must now exercise independent judgment on what a statute means — they may no longer defer to an agency just because a law is ambiguous. The agency’s view counts only as informed judgment that can persuade, not control. Vote: 6–3 (Roberts majority, Kagan dissent). What it did not do: invalidate any particular rule, or revive the nondelegation doctrine — the Court has struck down a statute on nondelegation grounds exactly twice, both in 1935, and not since.

The ten rules

Three were killed by Congress, three were killed or partly killed by the courts, one was partly repealed by the agency that wrote it, and three are still in force. One of the three in force is the exception that proves the rule: Congress voted the core of the airline refund rule into law three weeks after the agency wrote it.

1. The ATF pistol-brace rule — vacated by a court  VACATED

“Factoring Criteria for Firearms With Attached ‘Stabilizing Braces’”

Citation: 88 FR 6478 (Jan. 31, 2023). Docket ATF 2021R-08F, RIN 1140-AA55. Amended “rifle” in 27 CFR 478.11 and 479.11, so a pistol with an attached stabilizing brace counts as a rifle (and, with a barrel under 16 inches, a short-barreled rifle) when factoring criteria indicate it was designed to be fired from the shoulder.

Who wrote it: Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives. Director Steven Dettelbach — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed July 12, 2022, 48–46. No longer in the job (resigned Jan. 17, 2025). Background: law — career federal prosecutor (DOJ Civil Rights Division, three U.S. Attorney’s offices), U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of Ohio 2009–2016, then partner at BakerHostetler. The rule itself was signed by Attorney General Merrick Garland on Jan. 13, 2023.

The law it claims: National Firearms Act, 26 U.S.C. § 5845(c) (“rifle”) and § 5845(a)(3) (short-barreled rifle); Gun Control Act, 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(7) and (a)(8). The honest line: those statutes define “rifle” — they never mention stabilizing braces. ATF’s reading turned a pistol with a brace into a rifle.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the National Firearms Act in 1934 and the Gun Control Act in 1968 — not on this rule. Congress did try to kill it: CRA disapproval H.J.Res. 44 passed the House 219–210 (June 13, 2023) but failed in the Senate 49–50 (June 22, 2023).

CRA status: Major rule. The GAO flagged that it lacked the CRA-required 60-day delayed effective date — it took effect Jan. 31, 2023, the day it was published.

What the courts did: Mock v. Garland — the 5th Circuit (Aug. 1, 2023) reversed the denial of a preliminary injunction, finding the final rule “not a logical outgrowth” of the proposal. The Northern District of Texas (Judge O’Connor) vacated the rule nationwide on June 13, 2024 (arbitrary and capricious, and not a logical outgrowth). The government’s appeal was dismissed by joint stipulation July 17, 2025 — the vacatur is final. The rule is unenforceable. ATF calls it the “Vacated Final Rule 2021R-08F.”

What it cost: ATF estimated $266.9 million/year (at a 7% discount rate; $245.6M at 3%), based on ~3 million braces in circulation. Disputed: CRS estimated 10–40 million braces; a 25-state lawsuit cited ATF’s own analysis for $2–5 billion in total private-sector cost.

Dates: Proposed rule 86 FR 30826 (June 10, 2021); 90-day comment period to Sept. 8, 2021; over 237,000 comments received (about 92% opposed). Effective Jan. 31, 2023. 120-day compliance deadline May 31, 2023 (register tax-free, remove the brace, fit a 16-inch barrel, surrender, or destroy).

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): Vacated nationwide. ATF proposed deleting the 2023 language (91 FR 24453, May 6, 2026; comments closed Aug. 4, 2026); no final rescission published yet. The text technically remains printed in the CFR pending rescission.

Sources: Federal Register 88 FR 6478 and 91 FR 24453; GAO major-rule report B-335007; Congress.gov H.J.Res. 44; Mock v. Garland, 75 F.4th 563 (5th Cir. 2023); N.D. Tex. vacatur order, June 13, 2024.

2. The EPA power-plant rule — never decided on the merits, then partly repealed  PARTLY REPEALED

New Source Performance Standards for greenhouse-gas emissions from fossil fuel-fired power plants

Citation: 89 FR 39798 (May 9, 2024). Docket EPA-HQ-OAR-2023-0072, RIN 2060-AV09. 40 CFR 60, subparts TTTT/UUUUa. Repealed the Affordable Clean Energy Rule; set 90%-capture carbon-capture standards for long-term coal units (compliance Jan. 1, 2032) and new baseload gas turbines, plus 40% gas co-firing by 2030 for medium-term coal units.

Who wrote it: Environmental Protection Agency. Administrator Michael S. Regan — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed March 10, 2021, 66–34. No longer in the job (resigned Dec. 31, 2024); succeeded by Lee Zeldin (since Jan. 29, 2025). Background: career environmental regulator — EPA staff 1998–2008, Environmental Defense Fund 2008–2017, Secretary of the North Carolina Department of Environmental Quality 2017–2021. No industry background.

The law it claims: Clean Air Act § 111 (42 U.S.C. § 7411) — § 111(b) for new/modified sources, § 111(d) for existing sources, § 111(a)(1) for the “best system of emission reduction” definition. The honest line: the § 111 text Congress enacted never mentions greenhouse gases or carbon capture.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. CRA resolutions were introduced June 5, 2024 — S.J.Res. 92 died in the Senate Environment and Public Works Committee with no vote; H.J.Res. 163 was ordered reported by House Energy & Commerce 23–18 (Sept. 18, 2024) but never got a floor vote. Both died with the 118th Congress.

CRA status: Major rule. 60-day delay observed — effective July 8, 2024.

What the courts did: West Virginia v. EPA, No. 24-1120 (D.C. Cir.), filed May 9, 2024 by 25 states led by West Virginia plus industry groups. D.C. Circuit denied a stay July 19, 2024; the Supreme Court denied a stay Oct. 16, 2024 — Justice Kavanaugh (joined by Gorsuch) said challengers showed “a strong likelihood of success on the merits as to at least some of their challenges” but no irreparable harm before June 2025; Justice Thomas would have granted the stay. Oral argument Dec. 6, 2024. No merits decision was ever issued; the case was held in abeyance (Feb. 19 and Apr. 25, 2025) pending EPA’s new rulemaking. Precedent: West Virginia v. EPA, 597 U.S. 697 (2022), 6–3, Roberts — § 111(d) did not authorise generation-shifting caps; major questions require clear congressional authorisation.

What it cost: EPA’s Regulatory Impact Analysis: 1.38 billion metric tons of CO2 avoided through 2047; compliance costs ~$7.5–19 billion; ~$270 billion in climate benefits + ~$120 billion in health benefits; net benefits $320–370 billion. Core dispute: challengers said 90% carbon capture was not “adequately demonstrated” and threatened reliability; EPA said it was proven, at-the-source technology — not the grid-wide generation shifting the Supreme Court barred in West Virginia v. EPA (2022).

Dates: Proposed rule 88 FR 33240 (May 23, 2023), comments to Aug. 8, 2023; supplemental proposal 88 FR 80682 (Nov. 20, 2023). Comment count: Unverified as a final total — best sourced figure is 1,293,352 received as of Oct. 24, 2023 (a running count, not a final total). Effective July 8, 2024.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): Partly repealed. EPA under Zeldin finalised a partial repeal Sept. 14, 2026 (91 FR 58954, effective Nov. 16, 2026) — repealing the existing-unit guidelines, the carbon-capture standards for modified coal units and new baseload gas turbines, and the 40% co-firing requirement. A supplemental proposal (91 FR 59002, comments due Nov. 2, 2026) argues § 111 does not authorise regulating power-plant greenhouse gases for global climate change at all, and proposes repealing everything remaining.

Sources: Federal Register 89 FR 39798 and 91 FR 58954; reginfo.gov RIN 2060-AV09; EPA May 2024 RIA; Congress.gov S.J.Res. 92 / H.J.Res. 163; Harvard EELP tracker; West Virginia v. EPA, 597 U.S. 697 (2022).

3. The HHS Section 1557 rule — the gender-identity provisions were vacated  PARTLY VACATED

Nondiscrimination in Health Programs and Activities (Section 1557)

Citation: 89 FR 37522 (May 6, 2024). RIN 0945-AA17, docket HHS-OS-2022-0012. Amends 42 CFR Parts 438/440/457/460 and 45 CFR Parts 80/84/92/147/155/156.

Who wrote it: Department of Health and Human Services (Office for Civil Rights + Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services). Secretary Xavier Becerra — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed March 18, 2021, 50–49. No longer in the job (served to Jan. 20, 2025); succeeded by Robert F. Kennedy Jr., confirmed Feb. 13, 2025, 52–48. Background: law/politics — 12 terms in the U.S. House, then California Attorney General.

The law it claims: Affordable Care Act § 1557 (42 U.S.C. § 18116), incorporating Title VI, Title IX, the Age Discrimination Act, and Rehabilitation Act § 504. The honest line: the statute prohibits discrimination “on the basis of sex” — it never mentions gender identity. The rule read “sex” to include gender identity by way of the Supreme Court’s Bostock decision; that reading is what the courts struck down.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the Affordable Care Act in 2010 — not on this rule. CRA resolutions were introduced (S.J.Res. 90 and H.J.Res. 160, both May 23, 2024); both died in committee. Neither got a vote; no disapproval was enacted.

CRA status: Major rule (GAO report May 21, 2024). 60-day delay observed — effective July 5, 2024.

What the courts did: Three orders on July 3, 2024, two days before effectiveness. Tennessee v. Becerra/Kennedy (S.D. Miss., 15 states) — nationwide preliminary injunction July 3, 2024; Oct. 22, 2025 final judgment: summary judgment for the plaintiffs, universal vacatur of the gender-identity provisions (807 F. Supp. 3d 613) + a declaration that HHS exceeded its statutory authority. Texas v. Becerra (E.D. Tex.) — nationwide stay of the “on the basis of sex” definition, still in effect per an April 2026 status report. Florida v. HHS (M.D. Fla.) — injunction within Florida only. Net: the gender-identity provisions are vacated and unenforceable nationwide; the rest of the rule (including the AI decision-support duties) remains in force per HHS.

What it cost: HHS’s Regulatory Impact Analysis (2022 dollars): Year 1 $1,102.9 million (training $927.4M largest); ongoing $511.7 million/year (including $136.6M/year for gender-affirming-care coverage from year 2). Benefits unquantified by HHS. Disputed: one commenter estimated $16.4 billion over 5 years for transition-related coverage alone (up to ~$170 billion on broader assumptions) vs. HHS’s ~$0.68 billion over 5 years — a commenter’s estimate, not an official figure.

Dates: Proposed rule 87 FR 47824 (Aug. 4, 2022). Comment period ended Oct. 3, 2022. 85,280 comments received. Effective July 5, 2024, with phased applicability dates (some provisions up to a year; decision-support-tool duties May 1, 2025).

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): No 2025 proposal to revise or rescind the 2024 rule was published. Instead: May 14, 2025 rescission of the 2021 Section 1557 Bostock guidance; June 1–2, 2026 HHS Notice of Vacatur (91 FR 32887) confirming the vacated provisions are “legally void” and will not be enforced; 2026 Unified Agenda lists RIN 0945-AA32, a proposal to modify the Section 1557 regulations, NPRM expected Feb. 2027 (reginfo.gov; a Manatt summary chart lists July 2027 — discrepancy flagged).

Sources: Federal Register 89 FR 37522 and 91 FR 32887; GAO major-rule report, May 21, 2024; Congress.gov S.J.Res. 90 / H.J.Res. 160; Tennessee v. Kennedy, 807 F. Supp. 3d 613 (S.D. Miss. 2025); HealthCare.gov court-decisions summary; reginfo.gov RIN 0945-AA32.

4. The IRS digital-asset broker rule — Congress killed this one  CONGRESS KILLED THIS ONE

“Gross Proceeds Reporting by Brokers That Regularly Provide Services Effectuating Digital Asset Sales”

Citation: 89 FR 106928 (Dec. 30, 2024), Treasury Decision 10021. RIN 1545-BR39. 26 CFR Part 1, § 1.6045-1. It required certain decentralized-finance (DeFi) participants — above all providers of trading front-end services in a position to know the nature of a customer’s digital-asset sale — to be treated as brokers and to file information returns and furnish payee statements (Form 1099-DA) reporting gross proceeds.

Who wrote it: Department of the Treasury, Internal Revenue Service. IRS Commissioner Danny Werfel — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed 54–42 on Mar. 9, 2023; gone Jan. 20, 2025. Background: government/consulting — career at the Office of Management and Budget (Controller 2011–2013; Acting IRS Commissioner 2013), then a leader of Boston Consulting Group’s Public Sector practice. Under Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen (confirmed 84–15, Jan. 25, 2021; background: academia/government — labor economist, Chair of the Federal Reserve 2014–2018, professor at UC Berkeley and Harvard). The rule was signed by Deputy Commissioner Douglas W. O’Donnell and approved by Deputy Assistant Secretary Aviva Aron-Dine.

The law it claims: Internal Revenue Code § 6045 (26 U.S.C. § 6045), especially § 6045(c)(1) as amended by § 80603 of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (2021); also § 7805(a). The honest line: the statute never mentions DeFi, decentralized finance, trading front-end services, or non-custodial front ends. What § 6045(c)(1)(D) actually says is that a broker includes “any person who (for consideration) is responsible for regularly providing any service effectuating transfers of digital assets on behalf of another person.” The IRS read “effectuating” to cover front-end providers who never take custody of a customer’s assets. Whether that reading was correct was never decided by a court.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act in 2021 — not on this rule. But Congress did something rarer: it voted the rule down. CRA disapproval H.J.Res. 25 passed the House 292–132 (Mar. 11, 2025) and the Senate 70–28 (Mar. 26, 2025).

CRA status: Major rule. 60-day delay not observed — GAO stated flatly the rule “does not have the required 60-day delay in its effective date” (effective Feb. 28, 2025). Disapproval signed by the President Apr. 10, 2025 as Public Law 119-5 — the rule “shall have no force or effect.”

What the courts did: Challenged, never decided on the merits. Blockchain Association, Texas Blockchain Council, and DeFi Education Fund v. IRS, No. 3:24-cv-03259-X (N.D. Tex., filed Dec. 27, 2024) alleged the rule exceeded Treasury’s statutory authority and violated the APA. After Pub. L. 119-5 was enacted, the parties stipulated to a voluntary dismissal without prejudice on Apr. 16, 2025, because the case had become moot. No court ever ruled on whether the IRS’s reading of “broker” was lawful.

What it cost: Treasury/IRS gave no dollar cost-benefit figure — it stated tax regulatory actions are not subject to the regulatory-impact-assessment requirements of Executive Order 12866. What it did estimate, under the Paperwork Reduction Act, was a time burden: about 360 hours per year per covered entity, with start-up costs of 1,800 to 3,600 hours. The other side of the ledger (labelled as theirs): the Joint Committee on Taxation estimated repealing the rule would reduce federal receipts by about $3.9 billion over FY 2025–2034 — the tax revenue the rule was expected to produce through better reporting, not a compliance cost. Industry plaintiffs argued the rule would “effectively end the DeFi industry” in the U.S., but put no competing dollar figure on that claim.

Dates: Proposed rule 88 FR 59576 (Aug. 29, 2023); comments due Oct. 30, 2023, extended to Nov. 13, 2023; over 44,000 written comments received. (Regulations.gov showed over 125,000 “submissions,” but that figure counted submissions commenters self-reported as included in their comment, not comments actually received.) Final rule published Dec. 30, 2024; effective Feb. 28, 2025. The DeFi provisions applied to sales on or after Jan. 1, 2027 — no 1099-DA reporting under this rule was ever due.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): KILLED. Congress disapproved it under the Congressional Review Act and the President signed the disapproval into law as Public Law 119-5 on Apr. 10, 2025. Treasury and the IRS then formally removed it from the Code of Federal Regulations, effective July 11, 2025 (90 FR 30825). Under the CRA, Treasury/IRS may not reissue the rule in substantially the same form without a new law from Congress. The separate July 2024 custodial-broker rule (T.D. 10000) was not part of the disapproval — custodial brokers’ Form 1099-DA reporting remains in place.

Sources: Federal Register 89 FR 106928 and 90 FR 30825; GAO major-rule report B-336975; Congress.gov H.J.Res. 25; Public Law 119-5; Joint Committee on Taxation JCX-11-25.

5. The CFPB overdraft rule — Congress killed this one  CONGRESS KILLED THIS ONE

“Overdraft Lending: Very Large Financial Institutions”

Citation: 89 FR 106768 (Dec. 30, 2024). Docket CFPB-2024-0002, RIN 3170-AA42. 12 CFR Parts 1005 (Regulation E) and 1026 (Regulation Z). It narrowed the long-standing Regulation Z exceptions that kept overdraft fees out of the definition of “finance charge,” so overdraft credit from banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in assets would be treated as credit under the Truth in Lending Act — the institution had to either charge no more than its own breakeven costs and losses, charge no more than a $5 benchmark fee, or comply with Regulation Z’s disclosure, account-separation, and credit-card protections for that overdraft credit.

Who wrote it: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Director Rohit Chopra — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed 50–48 on Sept. 30, 2021; gone Feb. 1, 2025 (removed by President Trump). Background: government/regulator — he helped launch the CFPB, served as its Associate Director and first Student Loan Ombudsman (2011–2015), then served as a Commissioner of the Federal Trade Commission (2018–2021); before government he worked at McKinsey & Company. He signed the rule himself.

The law it claims: Truth in Lending Act § 105(a) (15 U.S.C. § 1604(a)); Electronic Fund Transfer Act §§ 904(a) and (c) (15 U.S.C. § 1693b); Consumer Financial Protection Act § 1022(b)(1) (12 U.S.C. § 5512(b)(1)); and § 2 of the CARD Act of 2009. The honest line: none of those statutes mentions overdraft fees, a $5 cap, a “breakeven” test, or “very large financial institutions.” The $5 benchmark and the breakeven method are the CFPB’s own creations. What the rule actually changed was a regulatory exception: when the Federal Reserve first adopted Regulation Z in 1969, it excepted charges for honoring overdrawn checks from “finance charge” — the CFPB narrowed that 55-year-old exception for large institutions.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the enabling statutes — TILA in 1968, EFTA in 1978, the CARD Act in 2009, Dodd-Frank in 2010 — not on this rule. But Congress did vote the rule down: CRA disapproval S.J.Res. 18 passed the Senate 52–48 (Mar. 27, 2025) and the House 217–211 (Apr. 9, 2025).

CRA status: Major rule. 60-day delay observed — published Dec. 30, 2024 with an effective date of Oct. 1, 2025, more than nine months later. Disapproval signed by the President May 9, 2025 as Public Law 119-10 — nearly five months before its effective date. The rule therefore never applied to anyone.

What the courts did: Challenged, never decided on the merits. Mississippi Bankers Association et al. v. CFPB, No. 3:24-cv-00792 (S.D. Miss., filed Dec. 12, 2024) alleged TILA does not authorize treating discretionary overdraft services as “credit” or imposing a price cap. The preliminary-injunction motion was still pending when Congress acted; the plaintiffs moved to dismiss as moot on May 13, 2025, and the court granted the dismissal in mid-May 2025. (The exact day of the dismissal order is Unverified to the day — secondary trackers disagree, May 16 vs. May 19.)

What it cost: The CFPB estimated a $5 benchmark fee would save consumers $5.2 billion annually and could reduce the revenue of very large financial institutions by the same $5.2 billion — a transfer (money consumers would keep that banks would not collect), not a net social cost, and the CFPB presented it as such. Disputed (labelled as theirs): the banking associations argued the $5 “breakeven” calculation undercounts the actual cost of offering overdraft services — derived from the charge-off losses of five very large institutions, omitting processing, customer service, compliance, core providers, and technology costs. They published no competing aggregate dollar figure.

Dates: Proposed rule 89 FR 13852 (Feb. 23, 2024); comments due Apr. 1, 2024; over 48,000 comments received (over 47,000 from individual consumers). Final rule issued Dec. 12, 2024; published Dec. 30, 2024. Effective date / compliance deadline: Oct. 1, 2025 — never reached.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): KILLED — and it never took effect. Congress disapproved it under the Congressional Review Act and the President signed the disapproval into law as Public Law 119-10 on May 9, 2025. Under the CRA, the CFPB may not reissue the rule in substantially the same form without a new law from Congress. The pre-existing Regulation E opt-in rule for ATM and one-time debit-card overdraft fees was not touched by this rule or its repeal.

Sources: Federal Register 89 FR 106768 and 89 FR 13852; GAO major-rule report B-337003; Congress.gov S.J.Res. 18; CRS Insight IN12513; Mississippi Bankers Assn. v. CFPB, No. 3:24-cv-00792 (S.D. Miss.).

6. The CFPB larger-participant rule — Congress killed this one  CONGRESS KILLED THIS ONE

“Defining Larger Participants of a Market for General-Use Digital Consumer Payment Applications”

Citation: 89 FR 99582 (Dec. 10, 2024). Docket CFPB-2023-0053, RIN 3170-AB17. 12 CFR Part 1090, new § 1090.109. It defined “larger participants” of the market for general-use digital consumer payment applications — nonbank companies that are not small businesses and facilitate at least 50 million covered consumer payment transactions a year — so the CFPB could supervise (examine) them for compliance with federal consumer financial law, the way it supervises large banks. It imposed no new substantive consumer-protection requirements of its own.

Who wrote it: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Director Rohit Chopra — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed 50–48 on Sept. 30, 2021; gone Feb. 1, 2025 (removed by President Trump). Background: government/regulator — he helped launch the CFPB, served as its Associate Director and first Student Loan Ombudsman (2011–2015), then served as a Commissioner of the Federal Trade Commission (2018–2021); before government he worked at McKinsey & Company. He signed the rule himself.

The law it claims: Consumer Financial Protection Act § 1024(a)(1)(B) and (a)(2) (12 U.S.C. § 5514(a)(1)(B), (a)(2)) — authority to supervise nonbank “larger participants of a market for other consumer financial products or services, as defined by rule[s]” the CFPB issues; § 1024(b)(7); § 1022(b)(1). The CFPA is Title X of the Dodd-Frank Act (2010). The honest line: the statute authorizes supervision of “larger participants” of a market the CFPB defines by rule — but it never mentions digital payment applications, digital wallets, person-to-person payment apps, or any transaction threshold. The market definition and the 50-million-transactions-a-year test are entirely the CFPB’s choice. (The proposed rule would have set the threshold at 5 million; the final rule raised it tenfold to 50 million.)

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the Dodd-Frank Act in 2010 — not on this rule. But Congress did vote the rule down: CRA disapproval S.J.Res. 28 passed the Senate 51–47 (Mar. 5, 2025) and the House 219–211 (Apr. 9, 2025).

CRA status: Not a major rule — the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs designated it “not a major rule,” so there is no GAO report for it and the 60-day delay did not apply. The rule took effect 30 days after publication (Jan. 9, 2025). Disapproval signed by the President May 9, 2025 as Public Law 119-11 — the same day Pub. L. 119-10 killed the overdraft rule.

What the courts did: Challenged, never decided on the merits. TechNet and NetChoice, LLC v. CFPB, No. 1:25-cv-00118 (D.D.C., filed Jan. 16, 2025) alleged the rule violated the APA. The CFPB agreed not to exercise any supervisory authority under the rule while the case was pending and never filed an answer. The plaintiffs filed a notice of voluntary dismissal on May 20, 2025, after Congress and the President invalidated the rule.

What it cost: The CFPB estimated the cost of supporting a supervisory examination at about $196,000 per larger participant per year, and no more than $1.4 million industry-wide per year even if half of the larger participants underwent an extended examination every year. The rule imposed no new recordkeeping, reporting, or disclosure requirements. For scale, the CFPB estimated the rule would cover about seven larger participants responsible for about 98% of transactions in the market — roughly 13.5 billion consumer payment transactions a year, worth about $1.2 trillion. The CFPB named no companies in the rule; CRS identified the seven it expected to be covered as Google Pay, Apple Pay, Samsung Pay, Venmo, PayPal, Cash App, and Facebook. Disputed (labelled as theirs): industry commenters argued supervision costs could disincentivize investment and might be passed through to merchants and consumers. No commenter put a competing aggregate dollar figure on the rule’s cost.

Dates: Proposed rule 88 FR 80197 (Nov. 17, 2023); comments due Jan. 8, 2024; 59 comments received. Final rule issued Nov. 21, 2024; published Dec. 10, 2024. Effective Jan. 9, 2025. No separate compliance deadline — the rule established supervisory authority as of that date.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): KILLED — after about four months in effect. The rule took effect Jan. 9, 2025 and was in force until Congress disapproved it under the Congressional Review Act; the President signed the disapproval into law as Public Law 119-11 on May 9, 2025, and the rule has had no force or effect since. Under the CRA, the CFPB may not reissue the rule in substantially the same form without a new law from Congress.

Sources: Federal Register 89 FR 99582 and 88 FR 80197; Congress.gov S.J.Res. 28; Public Law 119-11; CRS In Focus IF12935; TechNet & NetChoice v. CFPB, No. 1:25-cv-00118 (D.D.C.).

7. The CFPB medical-debt rule — vacated by a court  VACATED

“Prohibition on Creditors and Consumer Reporting Agencies Concerning Medical Information (Regulation V)”

Citation: 90 FR 3276 (Jan. 14, 2025). Docket CFPB–2024–0023, RIN 3170–AA54, 12 CFR Part 1022. It removed the “financial information exception” letting creditors use medical-debt information, and generally barred credit bureaus from furnishing medical-debt information to creditors.

Who wrote it: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Director Rohit Chopra — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed 50–48 on Sept. 30, 2021; gone Feb. 1, 2025 (removed by President Trump). Background: government/regulator — he helped launch the CFPB, served as its Associate Director and first Student Loan Ombudsman (2011–2015), then served as a Commissioner of the Federal Trade Commission (2018–2021); before government he worked at McKinsey & Company. He signed the rule himself.

The law it claims: 12 U.S.C. § 5512; Fair Credit Reporting Act §§ 604(g) (15 U.S.C. § 1681b(g)). The honest line: the statute cuts against the rule — § 1681b(g)(1) expressly permits coded medical-debt information to be furnished and used. That is why the court killed it.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970), the FACT Act (2003) and Dodd-Frank (2010) — never on this rule. CRA disapproval resolutions were introduced (S.J.Res. 36, Mar. 11, 2025; H.J.Res. 74, Mar. 6, 2025); both died in committee with no vote. Congress did not kill this rule — a court did.

CRA status: Major rule — GAO B-337010 (Jan. 29, 2025). 60-day delay observed in form (effective Mar. 17, 2025, 62 days after publication). No CRA disapproval was ever enacted.

What the courts did: Cornerstone Credit Union League v. CFPB, No. 4:25-cv-00016-SDJ (E.D. Tex., Judge Sean D. Jordan). Stayed Feb. 2025 (effective date postponed to June 15, 2025); Apr. 2025 the CFPB itself jointly asked for vacatur; July 11, 2025 the court vacated the rule in its entirety as exceeding CFPB authority and contrary to the Fair Credit Reporting Act, remanding to the CFPB. The rule never took effect.

What it cost: The CFPB said the rule would remove ~$49 billion in medical bills from ~15 million Americans’ reports, raise average credit scores about 20 points, and produce ~22,000 extra mortgages a year; its cost-benefit analysis claimed benefits with “limited costs.” Disputed (labelled as theirs): the American Bankers Association disputed the CFPB’s reading of its 2014 predictive-value study; debt collectors estimated an 8% annual revenue loss (both per CRS IF12169).

Dates: Proposed rule 89 FR 51682 (June 18, 2024); comments due Aug. 12, 2024 — over 74,000 comments received, ~2,000 unique letters. Final rule announced Jan. 7, 2025, published Jan. 14, 2025; effective Mar. 17, 2025 (never took effect). No separate phased compliance deadline was stated — Unverified.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): VACATED July 11, 2025. The rule never took effect.

Sources: Federal Register 90 FR 3276; GAO major-rule report B-337010; Congress.gov S.J.Res. 36 / H.J.Res. 74; CRS IF12169; Cornerstone Credit Union League v. CFPB, No. 4:25-cv-00016 (E.D. Tex.).

8. The EPA PFAS drinking-water rule — still in force  IN FORCE

PFAS National Primary Drinking Water Regulation

Citation: 89 FR 32532 (Apr. 26, 2024). Docket EPA-HQ-OW-2022-0114, RIN 2040-AG18. 40 CFR Parts 141 and 142. It set the first-ever federally enforceable drinking-water limits for six “forever chemicals” (PFAS) — PFOA and PFOS at 4.0 parts per trillion each (health goal: zero), PFHxS, PFNA and HFPO-DA (GenX) at 10 ppt each, plus a Hazard Index of 1 for mixtures of two or more of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA and PFBS — applying to community water systems and non-transient non-community water systems.

Who wrote it: Environmental Protection Agency. Administrator Michael S. Regan — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed 66–34 on March 10, 2021; gone Dec. 31, 2024. Background: career environmental regulator — EPA staff 2001–2008, Environmental Defense Fund 2008–2015, then Secretary of the North Carolina Department of Environmental Quality 2017–2021. No industry background. His name is on the rule’s signature block.

The law it claims: Safe Drinking Water Act § 1412 (42 U.S.C. §§ 300f, 300g-1, 300g-2, 300g-3, 300g-4, 300g-5, 300g-6, 300j-4, 300j-9, 300j-11). The honest line: the Safe Drinking Water Act never names PFAS — or any specific chemical. It sets a process: EPA makes a regulatory determination that a contaminant may warrant regulation, then proposes and then promulgates a National Primary Drinking Water Regulation. The live legal fight is about exactly that process: the challengers (and now EPA itself) say EPA proposed standards for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA and the Hazard Index mixture at the same time as only a preliminary determination to regulate them, violating the sequencing § 1412(b)(1)(E) requires.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the Safe Drinking Water Act in 1974 and the contaminant-regulation process in the 1996 amendments — never on the PFAS limits, the 4 ppt / 10 ppt numbers, or the 2029 deadline. No CRA disapproval resolution was introduced for this rule in the 118th or 119th Congress.

CRA status: Major rule — GAO report B-336287 (May 13, 2024). 60-day delay observed — effective June 25, 2024, exactly 60 days after publication.

What the courts did: American Water Works Association, et al. v. EPA, No. 24-1188 (D.C. Cir., filed June 7, 2024). Never decided on the merits as of Nov. 12, 2026. Feb. 7 – July 22, 2025: held in abeyance while the new administration reviewed the rule. Sept.–Dec. 2025: EPA reversed position and asked the court to vacate the four “Index PFAS” standards (PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, Hazard Index mixture) while continuing to defend the PFOA/PFOS limits. Jan. 21, 2026: a unanimous three-judge panel denied EPA’s motion for summary/partial vacatur — “the merits of the parties’ positions are not so clear as to warrant summary action.” The rule stays fully in place while the case proceeds. Feb. 19, 2026: EPA moved to sever and hold in abeyance the challenges to the Index PFAS standards; Mar. 19, 2026: denied. Sept. 18, 2026: oral argument held. No merits decision yet. No court has ruled the rule lawful or unlawful. No stay of the rule itself has ever issued. (Exact filing date of EPA’s partial-vacatur motion is Unverified — petitioners’ March 2, 2026 brief says Dec. 1, 2025; Harvard’s tracker dates it Sept. 11, 2025.)

What it cost: EPA’s own estimate: quantified annual costs $1,548.64 million per year; quantified annual benefits $1,549.40 million per year; quantified net benefits $0.76 million per year — essentially a wash on quantified figures alone. EPA also estimated ~29,858 fewer illnesses and 9,614 fewer deaths over the following decades. Disputed (labelled as theirs): the American Water Works Association’s July 2024 analysis put capital investment at $37.1–$48.3 billion, or $2.7–$3.5 billion per year annualized including operation and maintenance — roughly twice EPA’s estimate.

Dates: Proposed rule 88 FR 18638 (Mar. 29, 2023); comment period closed May 30, 2023; over 120,000 comments received. (The posted-vs.-received split is Unverified from a primary source — one secondary analysis counted 121,991 received and 1,522 posted.) Final rule signed April 8, 2024; published April 26, 2024. Effective June 25, 2024. Compliance deadlines: initial monitoring complete by April 26, 2027; comply with the limits by April 26, 2029.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): IN FORCE as written — all six PFAS limits and the April 26, 2029 compliance deadline. May 14, 2025: EPA announced it would keep the PFOA/PFOS limits, extend their compliance deadline to 2031, and rescind the Index PFAS limits. An announcement is not a rule — nothing changed on that date. May 20, 2026: EPA published two proposed rules (rescission of the Index PFAS limits, 91 FR 29413; compliance-deadline extension for PFOA/PFOS to April 26, 2031, 91 FR 29425). Comment period closed July 20, 2026. Neither proposal has been finalized as of Nov. 12, 2026. Until a final rule publishes, the 2024 rule stands exactly as written.

Sources: Federal Register 89 FR 32532, 91 FR 29413, 91 FR 29425; GAO major-rule report B-336287; EPA May 14, 2025 announcement; American Water Works Assn. v. EPA, No. 24-1188 (D.C. Cir.); CRS R46652.

9. The DOT airline refund rule — Congress voted the core into law  CONGRESS VOTED ON THIS ONE

“Refunds and Other Consumer Protections”

Citation: 89 FR 32760 (Apr. 26, 2024). Dockets DOT-OST-2022-0089 and DOT-OST-2016-0208, RIN 2105-AF04. 14 CFR Parts 259, 260 (new part), 262, and 399. It made airline refunds automatic — when a carrier cancels a flight or makes a “significant change” (3+ hours domestic, 6+ hours international, plus airport, connection or class changes) and the passenger rejects the alternative, the carrier must refund the fare, taxes and fees to the original payment method within 7 business days (credit card) or 20 calendar days (other payment), without the passenger having to ask — and it also required refunds for significantly delayed/lost bag fees and for ancillary fees paid but not provided, plus 5-year transferable travel vouchers when a passenger cannot travel because of a serious communicable disease.

Who wrote it: U.S. Department of Transportation. Secretary Peter Paul Montgomery Buttigieg — appointed by President Biden, Senate-confirmed 86–13 on February 2, 2021; gone Jan. 20, 2025. Background: politics — mayor of South Bend, Indiana, 2012–2020, 2020 Democratic presidential candidate; before that a McKinsey consultant (2007–2010) and a U.S. Navy Reserve intelligence officer with a tour in Afghanistan (2014). No airline-industry background. He signed the rule himself on April 1, 2024.

The law it claims: 49 U.S.C. §§ 40101(a), 40113(a), 41702, 41708, 41712, 42301; also implements the FAA Extension, Safety, and Security Act of 2016 (bag-fee refunds) and the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2018 (ancillary-fee refunds). The honest line: the main authority, 49 U.S.C. § 41712, bans “unfair or deceptive practices” in air transportation — it never mentions refunds at all. DOT read a refusal to refund a cancelled flight as an unfair practice. The bag-fee and ancillary-fee refund pieces were directly ordered by Congress (2016 and 2018 Acts).

Congress voted the core of this rule into law. The usual flag does not apply here — and the card says so plainly. Three weeks after the rule was published, Congress passed the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024, § 503, Pub. L. 118-63, signed May 16, 2024, codified at 49 U.S.C. § 42305, requiring carriers to give a full refund (fare, taxes, ancillary fees) for any cancelled or significantly delayed or changed flight when the passenger does not accept rebooking or a voucher/credit. DOT then issued a conforming final rule, 89 FR 65534 (Aug. 12, 2024). So the refund duty now rests on a statute Congress passed — repealing the rule alone would not repeal the refund right. The parts that remain rule-only are the 5-year travel-voucher scheme, some definitions, and the notification requirements.

CRA status: Major rule — GAO report B-336310 (May 15, 2024). 60-day delay not observed — effective June 25, 2024, less than 60 days from receipt by Congress. No CRA disapproval resolution was introduced. Instead of disapproving, Congress legislated on the same subject weeks later.

What the courts did: No court has ever ruled on this rule. It was never sued, never stayed, and never vacated. Do not confuse it with the separate DOT rule published four days later, “Enhancing Transparency of Airline Ancillary Service Fees,” 89 FR 34620 (Apr. 30, 2024) — the industry sued over that one, and the 5th Circuit vacated it en banc on Feb. 3, 2026. That vacatur does not touch the refund rule.

What it cost: DOT’s own estimate (millions of 2022 dollars per year): consumer time savings from clearer refund rights ~$3.8 million/yr; bag-fee + ancillary-fee refunds $16.0 million/yr in transfers to consumers, plus $7.1 million/yr in airline administrative costs; 5-year travel vouchers/credits $3.4 million/yr outside a pandemic, $482.0 million/yr during a pandemic; ticket refunds for cancelled/significantly changed flights — the transfer from airlines to consumers is unquantified in the rule; DOT called the administrative cost “de minimis.” No major published dispute over this rule’s cost figures was found.

Dates: Ticket Refund NPRM 87 FR 51550 (Aug. 22, 2022); public hearing Mar. 21, 2023; over 5,300 comments received. Final rule signed April 1, 2024; published April 26, 2024. Effective June 25, 2024. Compliance deadlines: ticket refunds, bag-fee refunds and ancillary-fee refunds — Oct. 28, 2024; travel credits/vouchers — April 28, 2025.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): IN FORCE. The refund provisions in 14 CFR Part 260 have been operative since Oct. 28, 2024, and the refund duty is also written into statute (49 U.S.C. § 42305). Two narrow qualifications — neither is a stay or vacatur: on Dec. 5, 2025 DOT announced enforcement discretion (90 FR 55999) for the refund/notification requirements where a flight is merely renumbered (same flight, new number, passenger rebooked without significant change or delay); on July 7, 2026 DOT extended that pause for one year, to July 7, 2027, while the “Refund III” rulemaking (RIN 2105-AF36) is pending. DOT’s December 2024 proposal for cash compensation for controllable delays was withdrawn Nov. 17, 2025 — that was only a proposal, never part of this rule.

Sources: Federal Register 89 FR 32760, 89 FR 65534, 90 FR 55999; GAO major-rule report B-336310; FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024, Pub. L. 118-63, § 503 (49 U.S.C. § 42305); Airlines for America v. DOT, No. 24-60231 (5th Cir.) (the separate fee-disclosure rule).

10. The OSHA silica rule — rules written in blood, upheld in court  IN FORCE

“Occupational Exposure to Respirable Crystalline Silica”

Citation: 81 FR 16286 (Mar. 25, 2016). Docket OSHA-2010-0034, RIN 1218-AB70. 29 CFR 1910.1053 / 1926.1153. It cut the silica permissible exposure limit to 50 µg/m³ (8-hour average), down from ~100 µg/m³ (general industry) and ~250 µg/m³ (construction/shipyards), plus exposure assessment, controls, medical surveillance, and recordkeeping.

Who wrote it: Occupational Safety and Health Administration, Department of Labor. The accountable head was Assistant Secretary David Michaels — the rule was prepared under his direction and signed by him. Appointed by President Obama, unanimously confirmed (sworn in Dec. 9, 2009), gone Jan. 2017. Background: academia/public health — epidemiologist, professor at George Washington University, MPH/PhD from Columbia; previously Assistant Secretary at the Department of Energy under President Clinton. His boss, Secretary of Labor Thomas Perez (confirmed 54–46, July 18, 2013; background: law/politics — Harvard Law, DOJ civil rights, Maryland labor secretary), did not sign the rule.

The law it claims: Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970, §§ 4, 6, 8 (29 U.S.C. §§ 653, 655, 657), operative provision § 6(b)(5). The honest line: the statute never mentions silica or any exposure number — the 50 µg/m³ limit was OSHA’s choice.

⚑ Congress never voted on this rule. Congress voted on the OSH Act in 1970 — never on this rule. No CRA disapproval resolution for this rule was introduced. (Contemporaneous CRA resolutions targeted sibling rules — H.J.Res. 87 on the persuader rule, H.J.Res. 88 on the fiduciary rule — not silica. In 2017 Congress did use the CRA against a different OSHA rule, the recordkeeping rule, H.J.Res. 83.)

CRA status: Major rule per GAO-16-564R (Apr. 11, 2016); GAO found OSHA complied procedurally; never disapproved.

What the courts did: North America’s Building Trades Unions v. OSHA, 878 F.3d 289 (D.C. Cir. 2017), decided Dec. 22, 2017. The court rejected all industry challenges and the unions’ challenge to the 30-day medical-surveillance trigger; it held OSHA arbitrary and capricious only in failing to explain omitting medical removal protection, remanding that one issue. The rule was not vacated; it stayed in force. (Whether Supreme Court review was sought after the D.C. Circuit decision is Unverified.)

What it cost: OSHA’s own estimate: ~$1,030 million/year; 642 deaths prevented/year (124 lung cancer, 325 silicosis and other nonmalignant respiratory disease, 193 renal) + 918 silicosis cases/year; ~$8.7 billion monetized benefits, ~$7.7 billion net. Disputed (labelled as theirs): American Foundry Society / Environomics — foundry costs $2.2 billion vs. OSHA’s $47 million (~50×); construction ~$4.9 billion/yr vs. OSHA’s ~$659 million/yr. (The $4.9 billion construction figure comes via trade-press reporting of the study, not the study itself.)

Dates: NPRM Sept. 12, 2013 (78 FR 56274); comment period extended twice, ended Feb. 11, 2014; over 2,000 comments received (GAO figure); hearings Mar. 18–Apr. 4, 2014, 200+ stakeholders, 70+ organisations; effective June 23, 2016. Staggered compliance: construction Sept. 23, 2017 (sample analysis June 23, 2018); general industry/maritime June 23, 2018 (action-level medical surveillance June 23, 2020); hydraulic fracturing engineering controls June 23, 2021.

Current status (checked Nov. 12, 2026): IN FORCE. Both standards live in the current CFR on osha.gov; all deadlines passed; not stayed, vacated, or rescinded. Loose end: the remanded medical-removal-protection provision still does not appear in the current § 1910.1053 text.

Why this rule is on this door: the deaths came first. The rule’s own preamble counts 16,305 silicosis death certificates from 1968–2002 (98% men); deaths fell from 1,157 in 1968 to 161 in 2005 but plateaued; OSHA says death certificates undercount silicosis; ~2.3 million workers exposed. NIOSH recommended a 50 µg/m³ limit in 1974 — 42 years before the rule. In industry the saying is that rules are written in blood. This one was.

Sources: Federal Register 81 FR 16285; GAO major-rule report GAO-16-564R; North America’s Building Trades Unions v. OSHA, 878 F.3d 289 (D.C. Cir. 2017); current 29 CFR 1910.1053 (osha.gov); OSHA silica background page; reginfo.gov RIN 1218-AB70.

How the chain works (what the door shows for every rule)

  1. The statute. What Congress actually passed, and when — and whether its text mentions what the rule does.
  2. The head. The unelected official who ran the agency: appointed by whom, Senate-confirmed or not, vote margin, still in the job or gone — and where they came from, so you can see whether the person regulating an industry ever worked in it.
  3. The rule. What it does in one sentence, what it costs, when it took effect, how many comments it drew.
  4. The check. Did Congress ever vote on it? Did a court strike it down, stay it, or uphold it? Is it still in force?

No grades. No verdicts beyond what a court actually ruled. No invented bios. Every claim carries its source.

Update cadence: monthly — a Federal Register pull for new major rules (GAO database cross-check), new CRA resolutions on Congress.gov, and status changes on these ten cards (court orders, repeals, effective dates). On an event — a court vacates or stays a card’s rule, a CRA resolution passes a chamber, or an agency proposes repeal — that card is updated within the week. Each card carries a “checked on” date. Cards checked on Nov. 12, 2026.

What’s next: this is the first sample — ten rules. The Oregon layer comes later, hand-built: Oregon’s rules database has no bulk download and its filings name a Rules Coordinator, not the agency director, so each Oregon card has to be joined to a roster by hand.

What this door does not do: there is no “tell Congress” action on the cards yet — that comes in a later phase, like the Bill Stripper’s constituent-forwarding. And the 2023 major-rule count is held until a direct check of the GAO database settles 76 vs. ~87.
